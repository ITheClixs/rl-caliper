\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Three levels of evidence. Policies small enough to enumerate, where the objective, the Fisher
information, the drift and both noise terms are computed rather than estimated. Transformers trained
from scratch, where a population of independent runs executes as one batched kernel so that sweeps
carry standard errors rather than anecdotes. And a pretrained model, where the same measurement is
made on a real policy and a real verifier. Everything ran on a single Apple M4 laptop.

\subsection{Exactly enumerable policies}

The policy is a tabular autoregressive model over a small vocabulary, so its distribution over
sequences, its score at every sequence, and the conditional moments $(p, u_1, S_0, S_1)$ are
available exactly. Propositions~\ref{prop:mean} and~\ref{prop:cov} were checked against
brute-force enumeration over all $|\mathcal{Y}|^{G}$ group outcomes for five estimators and
$G \in \{2,3,4\}$: agreement to $10^{-12}$. The scaled within-prompt term $(G-1)\tr\Sw$, which the
theory treats as $G$-independent, varies by $6\%$ over $G = 2$ to $32$.

\paragraph{The curvature model fails, the drift model does not.} Taking a real update and computing
the resulting improvement and KL exactly, the second-order model of the objective places its optimal
step at least four times below the step that actually maximises improvement, which is where the
sweep ended, because $J = \E[r]$ is
not a log-likelihood and a log-linear policy keeps improving past the turnover of a quadratic model.
The drift model \eqref{eq:drift} predicts the measured KL to between $2.6\%$ and $3.9\%$ across three orders of magnitude in step size. The theory in Section~\ref{sec:drift} is built on the second of these, not
the first.

\paragraph{Splitting a rollout budget.} For each prompt pool the parameter-free efficiency curve
$\rho(G)$ is compared against the improvement obtained by training at every split of a fixed budget,
holding total rollouts, total drift and step count fixed. Table~\ref{tab:exact-agreement} groups
pools by how much $\rho$ varies across the splits on offer.

\subsection{Transformers trained from scratch}

A population of independent transformers is warmed up on a verifiable running-sum task until each
member reaches a common pass rate, the noise terms are measured at that frozen policy, and every
member is then trained from the same starting point at each split of a fixed rollout budget. The
corpus size is the knob: sampling without replacement from a corpus of $N$ scales the between-prompt
term by $1 - (P-1)/(N-1)$, so shrinking the corpus toward the batch raises $G^{*}$ without touching
the task.

\subsection{What transfers when the batch size changes}

The sharpest consequence of the drift model is a statement about transfer. A run tuned by learning
rate is tuned in a unit that depends on the batch through $\mathcal{G} + N/P$; a run tuned by drift
is not. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} sweeps both parameterisations at four batch sizes and reports what
it costs to carry the value tuned at the smallest batch to the others.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \input{tables/transfer}
  \caption{Transfer across batch size. The learning rate that is optimal at $P = 8$ loses a fifth of
  the achievable gain by $P = 64$; the drift target that is optimal at $P = 8$ remains optimal.}
  \label{tab:transfer}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/transfer.pdf}
  \caption{Left: the optimum of each parameterisation relative to its value at $P = 8$. Centre and
  right: the sweeps themselves. The learning-rate optimum moves; the drift optimum does not.}
  \label{fig:transfer}
\end{figure}

The single-step drift model accounts for part of the movement in $\eta^{*}$ and not all of it: the
coefficient $\mathcal{G} + N/P$ measured at the initial policy predicts a smaller shift than the one
observed over a full run. The gap is itself the argument for measuring rather than scheduling, since
the coefficient changes as the policy improves and a controller tracks it while a fixed learning
rate cannot.

\subsection{A pretrained model}

The same measurement on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, with LoRA adapters as the trainable parameters and
corpora built from short exact-match families -- counting a letter in a word, naming the $k$-th item
of a list, adding two numbers -- plus a mixture of all of them. Families differ in how often the
model succeeds, and mixing them makes prompts pull in different directions, so the two knobs move the two noise terms separately. Table~\ref{tab:real} reports what the probe
measures, and shows Corollary~\ref{cor:bernoulli} failing across families: the ratio
$\tau_w/\E[p(1-p)]$, which the corollary says should be constant, varies by a factor of $8.8$.
That constant is the score scale $\sigma_s^2$, and a family whose answer is a single number does
not share one with a family whose answer is four numbers.

Measured optimal group sizes on this model run from $2.7$ to $6.2$, in the same range as
the enumerable policies and the transformers trained from scratch, and well below the eight to
sixty-four in common use. What closes that gap is not statistics but hardware, which the next
subsection measures.

\subsection{What the hardware is worth}

Equation~\eqref{eq:gstar-cost} inflates the optimal group size by
$\sqrt{1 + c_{\mathrm{pre}}/c_{\mathrm{dec}}}$, and both costs are properties of the serving stack.
Measuring them on this machine, for a $0.5$B model in bfloat16, gives a ratio that varies over three
orders of magnitude with the shape of the workload: prefill dominates when prompts are long and
responses short, and is negligible in the opposite case. Figure~\ref{fig:cost-model} converts the
measurement into the inflation it implies.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{figures/cost_model.pdf}
  \caption{Group-size inflation from sharing a prompt's prefill across its group, measured on an
  Apple M4. Long prompts with short responses justify groups several times larger than the
  statistical optimum; long responses do not.}
  \label{fig:cost-model}
\end{figure}

This is the practical resolution of the gap between measured optima of three to six and the eight
to sixty-four in common use. For short-answer verifiable tasks, where a long instruction is followed
by a few tokens of answer, prefill amortisation justifies most of the difference. For
reasoning-length responses it justifies almost none of it, and the statistical optimum stands.

\subsection{Carrying a recipe to a setting it was not tuned on}
\label{sec:recipe}

The previous subsections tune one quantity at a time. The question a practitioner actually faces is
whether the whole recipe survives a move, so we fixed two recipes on one setting and carried them,
unchanged, to another with four times the rollout budget, eight times the corpus and a different
seed. The default takes a common group size of sixteen and the learning rate that was best on the
tuning setting. The measured recipe takes the group size its probe reports on the held-out setting
and a drift target carried over from the tuning setting.

\textbf{The measured recipe lost}, by $28.9\%$ of the default's gain (paired $t = -3.01$ over $32$
runs). Because the two recipes differ in two ways at once, that result attributes nothing on its
own, so we crossed the two factors on the held-out setting: each group size swept over both a
learning-rate grid and a drift-target grid.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \input{tables/diagnosis}
  \caption{Best achievable gain at the held-out setting, crossing the group size against the rule
  that sets the step. Starred entries sit at the edge of their grid and are therefore lower bounds.
  The group size the theory prefers wins under both parameterisations; the loss above came from the
  drift target, not from the group size.}
  \label{tab:diagnosis}
\end{table}

Three things follow, and only the first is what we set out to show.

\emph{The group-size prediction holds at this setting.} $G = 4$, which the probe selects, beats
$G = 16$ under both parameterisations. Under drift control, where the step size is not a
confound, it wins by $11.1\%$ against a predicted $\sqrt{\rho_4/\rho_{16}} - 1 = 10\%$.

\emph{The drift target transfers across group size but not across this change of budget.} At the
held-out setting both group sizes have their optimum at the same drift target, $1.45 \times
10^{-3}$: the group size does not move it at all. The tuning setting's optimum was $3.19 \times
10^{-3}$, a factor of $2.2$ away, and using it costs $20\%$ of the achievable gain. Section
\ref{sec:experiments} showed the target holding to within $5\%$ across a fourfold change in batch
size and $21\%$ across an eightfold one; a sixteenfold change in prompts per step, combined with a
fourfold change in rollout budget, is past where it holds. The unit is right and the scope is
narrower than the earlier sweep alone would suggest.

\emph{A constant drift target is not the best schedule.} At both group sizes a tuned constant
learning rate beat the best constant drift target, and its optimum sat at the edge of the grid. A
fixed learning rate produces a drift that changes over a run as the gradient statistics change; the
fact that this beats holding drift fixed says the optimal drift schedule is not flat. That is a
statement about scheduling, not about the unit, and it is the most concrete thing this paper leaves
open.
