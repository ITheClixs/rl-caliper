\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Four levels of evidence, in decreasing order of what can be computed and increasing order of what
a practitioner would recognise. Policies small enough to enumerate, where the objective, the Fisher
information, the drift and both noise terms are computed rather than estimated, and where the
forecast can be checked against the quantity it predicts rather than against an estimate of it.
Transformers trained from scratch, where a population of independent runs executes as one batched
kernel so that sweeps carry standard errors rather than anecdotes. A pretrained model of half a
billion parameters. And Qwen2.5-7B, eight seeds, which is what this machine holds. Everything ran on
a single Apple M4 laptop with $24$\,GB of memory.

\subsection{Forecasting the spread of a reported number}
\label{sec:exp-forecast}

This is the experiment the paper is for, and its protocol is prospective by construction. In each
of $\numForecastSettings$ settings one run is trained and frozen; the states it passed through are carried backwards
through \eqref{eq:adjoint} to produce a forecast of the across-seed standard deviation of the final
pass rate; the forecast is recorded; and only then are the remaining seeds of the
$\numForecastSeeds$ trained and their spread measured. Nothing about the outcome is available when the prediction is made.

The enumerable policy is used because there the reported number is the exact pass rate rather than
an estimate of it, so the measured spread is the spread of the quantity itself and not of a finite
evaluation set. Settings vary two policy shapes (a vocabulary of three over three positions, and of
four over two), two prompt-pool diversities, three difficulty bands, the run length $T$, the prompt
count $P$ and the step size $\eta$, which between them move the realised spread over a factor of
$47$.

Table~\ref{tab:forecast} reports a median absolute error of $\numForecastMedian\times$ and a worst case of
$\numForecastWorst\times$, and the forecast lands inside the measured $95\%$ interval in $\numForecastInside$ of $\numForecastSettings$
settings. That last count says the point forecast is consistent with the interval a finite number
of seeds puts on the measured spread. It is not the coverage of an interval the forecast itself
produces, which is a quantity this method does not yet report.
Two things in the breakdown are worth noting. The two policy shapes agree with each other
at $\numForecastMedian\times$ against $1.10\times$, so the result is not a property of one tabular family.
And accuracy is flat in run length at the median but that consistency is not: $90$ of $96$ settings at
$\eta = 0.5$ against $72$ of $96$ at $\eta = 1.5$, which is the linearisation showing up exactly
where Section~\ref{sec:exp-linearity} says it should.

To make the deliverable concrete: one setting in the middle of the grid, at twenty-five updates,
thirty-two prompts, groups of eight and mixed difficulty, reports a pass rate of $0.502$. The
forecast made from that single run is a standard deviation of $1.11\times10^{-3}$; the value
realised over forty-eight independent runs is $1.07\times10^{-3}$, with a bootstrap interval of
$[8.6\times10^{-4},\, 1.3\times10^{-3}]$. What a reader would be handed is $0.502 \pm 0.002$ from
the seeds, before the evaluation's own term is added.

What the forecast costs is two gradient evaluations per update for the backward pass and one
scalar per update for the injected term. It never forms $S_T$, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:potential} it never needs a transpose.

\paragraph{What the backward pass is worth.} The expensive part of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:forecast} is carrying the adjoint back, two gradient evaluations per update. The
ablation that says whether it earns them is to hold $b$ at $\nabla M(\theta_T)$ throughout, keeping
the injected term and discarding everything the trajectory does to it.

It does not, at these horizons (Table~\ref{tab:transport}). The two agree to a median of $2\%$ in
the forecast itself (extremes $0.72$ to $1.05$), the median error is identical to two decimal
places, and both land inside the measured interval in the same $\numForecastInside$ settings. What carrying it back buys is the tail:
at $T=25$ and $\eta = 1.5$ the worst case is $1.58\times$ carried against $1.75\times$ held.

This is not a defect in the theory; it is the theory being informative about its own cost.
Section~\ref{sec:exp-spectrum} measures most of the spectrum of $A_t$ sitting within $10^{-4}$ of
one, and $\nabla M$ points into the slow part of it, so over tens of updates $\Phi_{T,t}$ is close
to the identity along that one direction. The practical reading is that a run of this length should
use the cheap estimator, which costs one extra pass per update, needs no Hessian-vector products
and does not depend on the noisy directional derivative Section~\ref{sec:exp-real} reports. The
recursion is
what tells you when to stop: when the spectrum of $A_t$ moves away from one, or the run is long
enough that a $1\%$ per-update discrepancy compounds.

\paragraph{The whole estimator family, not one of them.} The grid above uses RLOO. Repeating a
common sub-grid under the mean-baseline and standardised GRPO estimators gives the same picture
(Table~\ref{tab:estimator-forecast}). This matters more than a robustness check usually would,
because Proposition~\ref{prop:potential} is what makes the backward pass affordable and it is a
claim about the whole count-based family: if it held only for the estimator whose difficulty weight
is constant, the result would be a curiosity rather than a method.

\paragraph{Where the uncertainty comes from.} Restricting $\Sigma_t$ to $\Sigma_b$ or to
$\Sigma_w$ splits the same forecast by origin. The two shares are exact and sum to one, and across
$\numMemorySettings$ settings the rollout share has a median of $78\%$: over the grid as a whole,
which responses were sampled matters more than which prompts were drawn. It is monotone in group
size, running $93\%$ at $G=2$, $82\%$ at $G=4$, $65\%$ at $G=8$ and $46\%$ at $G=16$, so the
majority changes hands somewhere between groups of eight and sixteen rather than rollout sampling
dominating throughout. With prompts drawn independently, $\Sigma_b$ and $\Sigma_w$ are both divided
by $P$ and the mix is independent of the number of prompts: adding prompts buys a smaller error
bar, only larger groups change what the error bar is made of. That equality is a property of
independent sampling. Drawing distinct prompts from a corpus of $N$ multiplies $\Sigma_b$ alone by
$1 - (P-1)/(N-1)$, so a run that covers much of its corpus every step does shift the mix towards
rollout sampling as $P$ grows.

\paragraph{The uncertainty is made early, not late.} If the filter were sharp, the kernel $k_t$ of
\eqref{eq:kernel} would concentrate near the end of the run and reproducing a result would mean
reproducing only its tail. We looked for that and found the opposite. Over $\numMemorySettings$
settings, the number of final updates holding $95\%$ of the forecast variance is a median of
$\numMemoryFraction$ of the run against $0.95$ for an exactly uniform kernel, and it does not move
when the step size is raised sixteen-fold or when the run is started from a policy already trained
for $1200$ updates.

Where the kernel is not flat it tilts the other way. In $\numTiltedEarly$ of $\numMemorySettings$
settings more of the final variance was injected in the first half of the run than in the second,
with a median first-half share of $\numFirstHalfShare\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:forecast}, right). The
mechanism follows from the two measurements either side of it: with $\Phi_{T,t}$ close to the
identity along $\nabla M$, $k_t \propto \eta_t^2\Sigma_t/P$, and $\Sigma_t$ is largest early
because $\E_x[p(1-p)]$ is largest before the policy has learned anything. Early noise is both
bigger and no more forgotten than late noise.

The practical reading is the reverse of the one we went looking for. Reproducing a run means
reproducing all of it, and if anything the beginning matters more, which is also where a larger
batch would buy the most.

\subsection{Why the forecast rests on filtering, not accumulation}
\label{sec:exp-seeds}

\paragraph{The recursion predicts divergence; a single timescale does not.} The enumerable policy
admits three things at once: the exact linearised recursion \eqref{eq:lyapunov}, the exact Fisher
information, and as many independent stochastic trajectories as we care to run. That allows
candidate models of seed divergence to be scored against ground truth rather than against each
other. Over $\numPropagationSettings$ settings spanning prompt diversity, group size, batch size and
step size, we compare the propagated covariance against two alternatives: accumulation with no contraction, and
the single-timescale law an earlier version of this work relied on.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \input{tables/propagation}
  \caption{Predicted against measured divergence between independent runs in the enumerable
  policy, with $400$ trajectories per setting. Error is the ratio of predicted to measured,
  summarised as $\exp$ of the median absolute log ratio. $d$ is the diversity of the prompt pool:
  low $d$ means the prompts are alike, which is where the objective supplies the most curvature.}
  \label{tab:propagation}
\end{table}

The propagated recursion is accurate to $\numPropagatedPercent\%$ at the median and $1.2\times$ at
worst, everywhere in the grid. The interesting column is not the first one.

Accumulation, the model that says noise is added rather than filtered, is wrong by a factor
of $2.06$ on homogeneous prompt pools and by $3\%$ on heterogeneous ones, with a worst case of
$3.8\times$. That is not a caveat on the result; it is the result. Filtering is done by the
curvature of the estimator potential, and a pool of similar prompts is where that curvature is
consistent enough to matter. Where the prompts disagree with each other the mean field is nearly
flat, $A_t$ is nearly the identity, and a run genuinely does accumulate its noise. A reader who
trains on a narrow task distribution is in the first column; a reader who trains on a broad one is
in the second, and for them the simpler model is fine.

The single-timescale law is wrong by two orders of magnitude wherever there is a spectrum to
compress, and its one respectable column ($3.24\times$ at $d = 0.15$) is the case where contraction
is strong enough that one rate almost describes it. Section~\ref{sec:exp-spectrum} measures the
spectrum directly and says why the general case is hopeless for it.

\paragraph{The filtering requires a learning signal.} If divergence stops growing because the
shared objective pulls two runs together, then removing the objective while leaving the noise in
place should restore accumulation. We ran a population of transformers twice from identical warmed
parameters: once normally, and once with every reward replaced by an independent coin flip. The
rollouts, the prompt draws, the group size and the step size are the same in both, and so is the
estimator; only the reward differs, and a count-based advantage depends on the pattern of rewards
within a group rather than on whether that pattern is deserved.

The two conditions separate completely. With a signal, divergence rises for ten updates, turns
over, and ends at $\numNullLearning$ of where it started while the pass rate climbs from $\numNullPassStart$ to $\numNullPassEnd$.
Without one it grows by a factor of $\numNullCoin$ over the same $\numNullUpdates$ updates and the pass rate does not
move. At the end of the run the two differ by a factor of $\numNullGap$.

The control that matters is the step size. Mean realised drift per update is
$6.10\times10^{-3}$ in the learning condition against $5.17\times10^{-3}$ in the null: the runs that
end up closer together are the ones that moved \emph{further} per update. Whatever brings them back
is not smaller steps.

\paragraph{On a pretrained model.} The transformers above are small and their task is synthetic.
We repeated the measurement on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with LoRA adapters, six runs differing only in
rollout randomness on exactly verifiable short-answer problems; because LoRA adapters start at zero
the runs begin from an identical policy by construction, and every checkpoint is scored on one fixed
batch of sequences.

\paragraph{Bounded, not proven stationary.} Across the settings in which a run is still learning,
the log-log slope of divergence against update count runs from $-1.15$ to $+0.26$, against the
$+1$ that accumulation requires. That rejects accumulation. It does not establish stationarity in
the strict sense: $\mathcal{J}_t$, $\Sigma_t$ and $\eta_t$ all move during training, so the level that
\eqref{eq:unrolled} converges to is itself slowly varying, and we describe the divergence as
bounded rather than stationary throughout.

\paragraph{How the level scales.} Compared at matched pass rate rather than matched step count,
a log-log fit over nine settings gives an exponent of $-0.61$ in the batch, $95\%$ CI
$[-1.42, +0.24]$, and $+0.59$ in the drift target, $95\%$ CI $[+0.27, +1.04]$, with $R^2 = 0.70$.
Both intervals contain the values $-1/2$ and $+1/2$ that follow from
$Q_t \propto \eta^2\Sigma/P$, and the batch interval also contains zero. Nine settings are too few
to pin a two-dimensional scaling law, and we report the intervals rather than the point estimates
for that reason.

\paragraph{Why the route through policy divergence was abandoned.} An earlier version of this work
predicted that the spread in final task performance would follow $\sqrt{\KL_\infty}$. It does not,
in our data: the fitted exponent is $+0.10$ with $95\%$ CI $[-0.03, +0.45]$ against a predicted
$+0.5$, and the interval contains zero. Policy divergence and outcome spread are positively
associated across our settings, but these experiments do not establish the predicted relation, and
we withdraw it.

The reason is structural rather than statistical, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-scale} shows it in its
extreme form. $\KL$ weights every direction the policy can move in by the Fisher information; a
task metric weights them by $\nabla M$, which is a single direction and can be nearly orthogonal to
where the variance is, or, on a saturated evaluation, zero. No scalar function of $\KL$ can
recover that. Theorem~\ref{thm:adjoint} takes the direct route instead, and
Section~\ref{sec:exp-forecast} is what it delivers.

\subsection{The filter is not a contraction}
\label{sec:exp-spectrum}

It is convenient to say that the updates following a perturbation shrink it. Measuring the spectrum
of $A_t = I + \eta\mathcal{J}_t$ along a run says something more careful, and the more careful
statement is the one the paper should make.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \input{tables/spectrum}
  \caption{The spectrum of the transfer operator along a run of $600$ updates at $\eta = 1$,
  reported as $10^3(\rho - 1)$ for the spectral radius $\rho$. The operator expands in its leading
  direction throughout, and contracts in a minority of directions that grows as the run proceeds.}
  \label{tab:spectrum}
\end{table}

Three things are visible in Table~\ref{tab:spectrum} and none of them is a uniform contraction.

The spectral radius exceeds one everywhere we look, by $1$--$2\%$ early and decaying towards
$1.0001$ as the policy improves. There are directions in which a perturbation grows. Over a run of
twenty-five updates that growth is a factor of $1.02^{25} \approx 1.6$, which is why divergence
stays bounded over the horizons anyone trains and why it would not stay bounded forever: what makes
the spread finite here is that expansion is slow relative to run length, not that every direction
shrinks.

The share of directions that contract at all is between $31\%$ and $51\%$, and it rises as the run
proceeds, from $36\%$ to $51\%$ on the mixed pool. The filter changes shape during training. This is
the non-stationarity that Theorem~\ref{thm:propagation} handles by construction, since it carries
$A_t$ rather than an average of it.

Among the directions that do contract, the fastest has an eigenvalue of
$\numSpectrumFastest$ and the slowest is $1.0000$ to four places. That is a range of decay
timescales from roughly fifty updates to effectively unbounded, in the same operator, at the same moment. It is the direct explanation of two
results reported above: why replacing the spectrum by one effective timescale is wrong by two orders
of magnitude, and why the per-update kernel has no horizon: the slow end of the spectrum has not
forgotten anything by the end of an eighty-update run.

We therefore say that noise is \emph{filtered} rather than contracted, and mean by it that
\eqref{eq:unrolled} reweights each update's contribution by a spectrum straddling one, most of
which is very close to it.

\subsection{How far the linearisation can be pushed}
\label{sec:exp-linearity}

Everything above linearises the mean update field between the two runs. That assumption is
checkable without knowing the measured spread, though it needs a pair of runs rather than one. For a
pair of runs at $\theta^A$ and $\theta^B$ with $\Delta = \theta^B - \theta^A$, define the residual
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:residual}
  \varepsilon \;:=\;
  \frac{\big\|\,\gbar(\theta^B) - \gbar(\theta^A) - \mathcal{J}(\theta^A)\Delta\,\big\|}
       {\big\|\,\gbar(\theta^B) - \gbar(\theta^A)\,\big\|},
\end{equation}
zero where the field is linear between them. We measure its median along $36$ pairs of runs and
report the forecast error against it.

The residual behaves as it should: raising the step size eightfold raises it from $0.028$ to
$0.165$. The forecast does not follow. Its median error is $\numForecastMedian\times$ where the residual is below
$5\%$, $1.18\times$ between $5$ and $12\%$, $1.08\times$ between $12$ and $25\%$, and $1.18\times$
above that, with a rank correlation of $0.17$ between residual and error across the grid. What does
track the residual is the tail: the worst case is $1.21\times$ below $5\%$ and around $1.57\times$
above it.

Two things follow. The derivation asks for more than the result needs, since a $16\%$ departure
from linearity costs nothing at the median, so the honest statement of the assumption is that it
bounds the worst case rather than setting the accuracy. And $\varepsilon$ is worth logging anyway,
because it is the quantity that separates the regime where the forecast has a worst case of
$1.2\times$ from the regime where it has one of $1.6\times$, and computing it costs one extra
gradient evaluation per checkpoint once a second run exists. Making it available before that
second run is what Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists first among the things we would build next.

\subsection{The exact moments the forecast is built on}

Every term the forecast injects comes from Propositions~\ref{prop:mean} and~\ref{prop:cov}, so
those are checked first. The policy is a tabular autoregressive model over a small vocabulary, so
its distribution over sequences, its score at every sequence, and the conditional moments
$(p, u_1, S_0, S_1)$ are available exactly. Propositions~\ref{prop:mean} and~\ref{prop:cov} were checked against
brute-force enumeration over all $|\mathcal{Y}|^{G}$ group outcomes for five estimators and
$G \in \{2,3,4\}$: agreement to $10^{-12}$. The scaled within-prompt term $(G-1)\tr\Sw$, which the
theory treats as $G$-independent, varies by $6\%$ over $G = 2$ to $32$.

\subsection{When the optimiser carries state}
\label{sec:exp-adam}

Adam's update depends on two moment estimates as well as the parameters, so a gradient perturbation
outlives the step that produced it and $\theta$ alone is no longer a closed state. The recursion
lifts: with $z = (\theta, m, v)$ and $\Phi_z$ the mean Adam map, the transfer operator is
$\partial\Phi_z/\partial z$ and the injection is $B\,\Sigma_t B^{\!\top}$ with
$B = \partial\Phi_z/\partial \hat{g}$, the sensitivity of one update to the gradient it was handed.
Both are taken by central differences on the exact mean map, so nothing about the derivation has to
be trusted.

One caveat is structural. Adam's map is singular at $v = 0$: the derivative of
$1/(\sqrt{v} + \epsilon)$ diverges there, and a recursion started at initialisation is not
linearisable at all. We warm the moments on mean gradients before propagating, which every run does
anyway, and every seed shares the warmed state so the covariance still starts at zero.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \small
  \input{tables/adam_lift}
  \caption{Predicted against measured seed divergence under Adam, over $24$ settings. The
  parameter-only row uses the same operators restricted to their $\theta$ block, so it isolates the
  cost of pretending the optimiser state does not carry a perturbation forward.}
  \label{tab:adam-lift}
\end{table}

The optimiser state is where almost all of the difficulty lives. Ignoring it underpredicts by
$24.6\times$ at the median, which is barely better than assuming noise accumulates without any
filtering at all ($24.2\times$): a recursion on $\theta$ alone cannot see that a perturbation to
one gradient is still moving the parameters twenty updates later through $m$ and $v$. Carrying the
full state brings that to $1.10\times$ at the median and $1.8\times$ at worst, and freezing the
second moment at its mean trajectory rather than propagating it costs little, $1.21\times$.

What matters more is that the lift no longer degrades where the metric saturates. Splitting by
pool, the median error is $1.06\times$ on the hard band, $1.13\times$ on the intermediate band
where the learning signal is live, and $1.10\times$ on the easy band where the pass rate reaches
$0.72$ and $\E_x[p(1-p)]$ is collapsing. The worst case on that easy band is $1.8\times$.

An earlier version of this work reported the opposite, a median of $8.0\times$ on the easy band and
a worst case two orders of magnitude out, and attributed it to Adam's normalisation being a
nonlinearity that a linear recursion cannot contain. That diagnosis was wrong, and the cause was
ours. Adam is handed a stochastic gradient, so the mean of its second-moment update is
$\beta_2 v + (1-\beta_2)\E[\hat{g}^2]$ with $\E[\hat{g}^2] = \gbar^2 + \operatorname{diag}\Sigma$.
Squaring the mean gradient instead, which is what we did, discards the gradient variance. On these
pools that variance is between $6.5$ and $15$ times the term that was kept, so $v$ was far too
small along the whole mean trajectory and every step taken from it was correspondingly too large.
The error was largest exactly where $\gbar \to 0$ and the variance is all that remains, which is
why it looked like a saturation effect. Correcting the mean map removes it.

The practical statement is therefore stronger than we could previously make: the recursion survives
an optimiser that carries state, at the same accuracy it has under plain ascent, provided the mean
map is the mean of the stochastic map and not the map evaluated at the mean gradient.

\subsection{The backward pass on a pretrained model}
\label{sec:exp-real}

We ran the forecast end to end on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with LoRA adapters, twelve updates of plain
gradient ascent, which is the update the recursion is written for, on a task the base model passes
at $0.16$, and afterwards trained seven more seeds and measured what they did. The backward pass is
the one in Algorithm~\ref{alg:forecast}, with the mean update field estimated from fresh rollouts
under common random numbers.

Two diagnostics came back before the comparison did, and both matter more than the comparison.

The first is that the run stops injecting almost immediately. The fraction of prompts whose group
is not unanimous falls from $0.5$ over the first four updates to essentially zero, so
$\Sigma_t \approx 0$ from update five onwards and the forecast is, in effect, made from four
updates out of twelve (Figure~\ref{fig:real-forecast}). This is the sampling collapse of
Section~\ref{sec:metric} happening at a step size we chose to be conservative, on a task chosen to
be hard for the model.

The second is that the directional derivative is not reliably estimated. Taking $J b$ twice from
independent draws of prompts gives two vectors whose cosine has a median between $0.07$ and $0.5$
depending on the run: mostly noise at this batch size. On its own that would sink the
method. It does not, because Section~\ref{sec:exp-forecast} has already established that the
transport is worth about $2\%$: what we report is the cheap estimator of \eqref{eq:cheap} in all
but name, and the quantity that is badly estimated is one the forecast barely uses.

\paragraph{What the comparison says.} The eight runs realise a spread of $0.0636$, of which
$0.0077$ is the evaluation's own binomial noise, leaving a seed term of $0.0631$ with an interval of
$[0.0350,\ 0.0735]$ (Table~\ref{tab:real-forecast}). The forecast a practitioner would run is the
cheap form of \eqref{eq:cheap}, which is what Section~\ref{sec:exp-forecast} recommends and what
the noisy directional derivative leaves available. It is $0.0025$, short by a factor of twenty-five.

Taking the transport anyway gives $0.0040$, and repeating the experiment says how far either
number can be trusted. Before the adapter initialisation was seeded, each launch of this
configuration started from its own draw, and those launches produced transported forecasts of
$0.0225$ and $0.0040$, a factor of $5.7$ apart, and cheap forecasts of $0.0056$ and $0.0025$, a
factor of $2.2$ apart. Neither form is stable under a change of initialisation, and the transported
one is far less so. The transport is worth $2\%$ where it can be computed exactly; here it is
estimated from vectors whose cosine with an independent estimate of themselves is between $0.07$
and $0.21$, and compounding twelve such corrections moves $\|b\|$ by an amount that does not
reproduce. On a real model the transport is not a small correction badly estimated, it is unusable,
and dropping it leaves a quantity that still moves by a factor of two between initialisations.

An explanation that would have been comfortable is ruled out by the same table. The injected term
is estimated from a batch of eight prompts, and once most groups are unanimous that sample is
mostly zeros, so it could be biased low. Re-estimating it from sixty-four prompts, from the same
initialisation and changing nothing else, moves the forecast from $0.0025$ to $0.0026$, about five
percent where a factor of twenty-five is needed. Measured on those sixty-four prompts the share of
prompts whose group is not unanimous still reaches zero at the fifth update and never exceeds five
percent afterwards, so the collapse is not a small-batch artefact either. The shortfall is not an
estimation problem.

The reason for the shortfall is visible without any statistics. The eight held-out scores are not
scattered around a centre. Three of them sit at $0.346$, $0.353$ and $0.354$, within $0.008$ of
each other, and the remaining five are spread from $0.204$ to $0.300$
(Figure~\ref{fig:real-forecast}, right). A linearised model predicts the spread of a unimodal
perturbation about one trajectory, and this is not that. Whether it is exactly two modes we would
not claim from eight runs, and the grouping is not identical between reruns; what is stable across
reruns is a tight cluster near $0.35$ sitting apart from everything else. A standard deviation is a
poor summary of such an outcome for anyone, forecast or not.

\paragraph{Whether the outcome is decided early, which we cannot establish.} It is tempting to read
the grouping off the start of each run, and the data invite it: the pass rate a run achieves on its
first three batches correlates with its final held-out score at $r = \numEarlyCorrelation$ here,
against $\numLateCorrelation$ for its last three. The bootstrap interval over runs is
$[\numEarlyCorrelationLow,\ \numEarlyCorrelationHigh]$, repeating the whole experiment moves the
point estimate between $+0.44$ and $+0.88$, and at seven billion parameters the same correlation is
$\numEarlyCorrelationBig$, which is to say absent. We report the numbers and draw nothing from
them. Eight runs of one configuration cannot support a claim of this shape, and we came close to
making one.

What the measurements either side of it do support is narrower. The per-update kernel puts
$\numRealFirstHalfShare\%$ of the forecast variance in the first half of the run, and
Section~\ref{sec:exp-spectrum} finds the leading eigenvalue of $A_t$ above one, so variance is
injected early and then amplified. Whether that is what decides which group a run ends in is the
step we cannot take, and it is the one that would have made the observation useful. The mechanism
the paper describes is visible on a real model; the magnitude the linearisation assigns to it is
not right, because the amplification carries the runs out of the regime where a linear model
applies.

\paragraph{Whether a better-behaved step size exists.} Not obviously, at this scale. A smaller step
keeps the runs closer and the linearisation valid, but then the metric barely moves and the seed
term drops below what eight runs can resolve at all (Appendix~\ref{app:power}: below about $0.01$
the subtraction returns nothing usable). The window where the runs both learn and stay in the
linear regime may simply be empty for a half-billion-parameter model on a task it finds hard, which
is a statement about the experiment we could afford rather than about the method.

\subsection{The forecast at seven billion, under the optimiser people use}
\label{sec:exp-adam-scale}

Section~\ref{sec:exp-real} makes the forecast under plain ascent, which is the update the
recursion is written for. A practitioner uses Adam. Section~\ref{sec:exp-adam} shows the lift to
$z = (\theta, m, v)$ holds where the moments are exact, so the remaining question is whether it
survives on a pretrained model, and this section answers it. Under the cheap form the injected
term enters through $B = \partial\Phi/\partial\hat{g}$, which for Adam is diagonal and analytic,
so the forecast is the same backward pass with the metric direction scaled by the optimiser's own
preconditioner at each update.

\paragraph{The measurement.} $\numAdamScaleSeeds$ runs of Qwen2.5-7B at four-bit precision, twelve
updates of Adam, take the held-out pass rate from $\numAdamScaleBase$ to $\numAdamScaleMean$. The
spread across runs is $\numAdamScaleSpread$, of which $\numAdamScaleBinomial$ is the evaluation's
own binomial noise, leaving a seed term of $\numAdamScaleResolved$ with an interval of
$[\numAdamScaleResolvedLow,\ \numAdamScaleResolvedHigh]$. No bootstrap draw lands at zero, so
unlike the $0.5$B design this one resolves the quantity it is measuring. The spread is a little
over half the improvement the runs achieved.

\paragraph{The forecast is short, by between $\numAdamScaleShortfallLow$ and
$\numAdamScaleShortfallHigh$.} It reads $\numAdamScaleForecast$ against a measured
$\numAdamScaleResolved$, a point ratio of $\numAdamScaleShortfall$. We do not report that ratio on
its own, because the same run's two half-sample estimates of the injected term are
$\numAdamScaleHalfLow$ and $\numAdamScaleHalfHigh$, a factor of $\numAdamScaleHalfRatio$ apart, and
the shortfall they imply runs from $\numAdamScaleShortfallLow$ to $\numAdamScaleShortfallHigh$. The
direction agrees with Section~\ref{sec:exp-real} and the size is now within the range a linearised
model might plausibly be wrong by, rather than the two orders of magnitude we previously reported.

\paragraph{A number we published and then corrected.} An earlier version of this section put that
shortfall at $\numAdamScaleStatelessShortfall$ and read it as evidence that the runs had left the
regime a local model can describe. The forecast behind it applied Adam's preconditioner to each
update's injection but then discarded the adjoint, carrying nothing from one update to the next.
The exact tier says what that costs: on the same grid, a recursion carrying no state is wrong by a
factor of $16.1$ in variance where the dynamics are perfectly local and the full lift is right to
$1.10\times$. Almost all of the $\numAdamScaleStatelessShortfall$ was therefore ours. Carrying
Adam's moments backwards on the identical trajectory and the identical prompt sample moves the
forecast from $\numAdamScaleStateless$ to $\numAdamScaleForecast$, a factor of
$\numAdamScaleStateGain$, and the conclusion the earlier number supported does not survive it.

\paragraph{What carrying the state costs, which is nothing.} The transfer operator on
$z = (\theta, m, v)$ has three blocks that require the Jacobian of the mean update, and all three
sit in the column that differentiates with respect to $\theta$. That Jacobian is the one quantity a
real model cannot supply: Section~\ref{sec:exp-real} measures independent estimates of it agreeing
to a cosine of about a tenth. Dropping those blocks leaves couplings that are diagonal and
analytic, costing one extra vector per update and no rollouts at all. On the exact grid it costs
nothing measurable, $1.10\times$ either way, so the accuracy the lift buys comes from carrying the
optimiser state and not from the curvature. That is convenient, since the curvature is the half we
could not have had.

\paragraph{The estimate of the injected term is now the binding constraint.} Carrying the state
concentrates the kernel on the first two updates, where a perturbation has the longest to act
through the moments, and those are estimated from the same sparse sample as everything else. The
half-sample disagreement accordingly rises from $2.4\times$ without the state to
$\numAdamScaleHalfRatio\times$ with it. The accuracy problem and the estimation problem have
swapped places: what limits this forecast is no longer the recursion but how well
$\numAdamScaleWidePrompts$ prompts pin down a variance when roughly a tenth of groups contribute
anything at all.

\paragraph{The diagnostic still costs more than it should.} That backward pass took $141$ minutes
against the $260$ that training all $\numAdamScaleSeeds$ seeds costs. The cost argument is
unchanged in kind by the correction, though the forecast it buys is now much closer to right. What
the correction does change is where the remaining effort belongs: on estimating the injected term
from the groups that actually carry it, rather than on the recursion, which is now the part that
works.

\subsection{Two ways a run stops being measurable}
\label{sec:exp-scale}

The largest model this machine holds is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at four-bit precision, trained with
LoRA adapters and Adam. Running it turned up two ways for a reported number to stop carrying
information, and they fail in opposite directions. In one the spread swamps the effect, so a single
run cannot report the improvement it claims. In the other the metric stops resolving the runs at
all, so the spread reads zero and a practitioner concludes their pipeline is reproducible. Both are
visible in \eqref{eq:adjoint}, which sends the spread of a reported number through $\nabla M$: the
first is that quantity being large next to what the run achieved, the second is $\nabla M$ vanishing
on the evaluated distribution.

\paragraph{The spread swamped the effect.} On counting a letter in a word, $\numScaleRuns$ runs of
$20$ updates take the held-out pass rate from $\numScaleBase$ to $\numScaleMean$ on average, an
improvement of $\numScaleGain$. The individual runs land between $\numScaleLow$ and $\numScaleHigh$,
a standard deviation of $\numScaleSpread$ across seeds, which is $\numScaleShare\%$ of the mean
improvement (Table~\ref{tab:scale}). The evaluation's own noise accounts for almost none of it.

The consequence is not that the training failed. On average it worked. It is that a single run of
this recipe reports a number whose run-to-run uncertainty is nearly as large as the effect being
claimed, so the ordinary practice of running once and comparing against a baseline cannot separate
a real improvement from a lucky seed at this scale. This is exactly the case the forecast of
Section~\ref{sec:metric} is meant for, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-adam-scale} supplies one for the
same model under the same optimiser, twelve updates rather than twenty.

\paragraph{The metric went blind.} On counting a letter in a word, a task the base model already
passes at $0.41$, twenty updates take the held-out pass rate to $\numSatScore$ and
drive every held-out prompt to a unanimous sample. All $\numSatPrompts$ of them return a measured
rate $\hat{p}$ of exactly $0$ or exactly $1$: eight samples, one answer. Eight samples cannot
distinguish $\hat{p} = 1$ from a true rate of, say, $0.9$, which is unanimous $43\%$ of the time,
so what is established is that the evaluation has stopped separating the runs, not that the
response distribution has collapsed to a point. Two runs differing only in rollout randomness
report \emph{the same number to the last decimal} and agree on
$\numSatPrompts$ of $\numSatPrompts$ prompts individually, while their policies differ by between
$1.8\times10^{-5}$ and $7.7\times10^{-2}$ in KL and that difference does not accumulate over the
run.

This is worth being precise about, because the two readings are opposite. It is not that the seeds
converged. It is that the metric stopped resolving them: the measured $\E_x[\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})]$
is zero on the evaluation set, so a score built from those samples has no variance to report,
whatever the policies do. The two readings are separated by the KL between the policies, which is
not zero, rather than by the scores, which are identical. The
practical consequence is that a zero measured spread on a saturated benchmark is not evidence of a
reproducible procedure, and the theoretical consequence is the one
Section~\ref{sec:metric} is built on: the spread of a reported number is
$\nabla M^{\!\top} S_T \nabla M$, and when $\nabla M$ is zero on the evaluated distribution no amount
of $S_T$ reaches it. It is also, in retrospect, why the route through $\sqrt{\KL}$ that an earlier
version of this work took could not have worked in general.
