\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Three levels of evidence. Policies small enough to enumerate, where the objective, the Fisher
information, the drift and both noise terms are computed rather than estimated. Transformers trained
from scratch, where a population of independent runs executes as one batched kernel so that sweeps
carry standard errors rather than anecdotes. And a pretrained model, where the same measurement is
made on a real policy and a real verifier. Everything ran on a single Apple M4 laptop.

\subsection{Forecasting the spread of a reported number}
\label{sec:exp-forecast}

This is the experiment the paper is for, and its protocol is prospective by construction. In each
of $48$ settings one run is trained and frozen; the states it passed through are carried backwards
through \eqref{eq:adjoint} to produce a forecast of the across-seed standard deviation of the final
pass rate; the forecast is recorded; and only then are $63$ further seeds trained and their spread
measured. Nothing about the outcome is available when the prediction is made.

The enumerable policy is used because there the reported number is the exact pass rate rather than
an estimate of it, so the measured spread is the spread of the quantity itself and not of a finite
evaluation set. Settings vary the prompt pool's difficulty band, the run length $T$, the prompt
count $P$ and the step size $\eta$, which between them move the realised spread over a factor of
$30$.

Table~\ref{tab:forecast} reports a median absolute error of $1.08\times$ and a worst case of
$1.67\times$. The forecast lands inside the measured $95\%$ interval in $36$ of $48$ settings,
which is below the nominal rate and worth being plain about: with $64$ seeds the measured interval
is narrow enough that a systematic modelling error of tens of percent shows up as a miss. The
accuracy does not degrade with run length --- $1.07\times$ at ten updates against $1.09\times$ at
twenty-five --- and degrades slightly with step size, which is what Section~\ref{sec:exp-linearity}
attributes to the linearisation.

What the forecast costs is two gradient evaluations per update for the backward pass and one
scalar per update for the injected term. It never forms $S_T$, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:potential} it never needs a transpose.

\paragraph{Where the uncertainty comes from.} Restricting $\Sigma_t$ to $\Sigma_b$ or to
$\Sigma_w$ splits the same forecast by origin. The two shares are exact and sum to one, and across
$288$ settings the rollout share has a median of $78\%$: which responses were sampled matters more
than which prompts were drawn. It is monotone in group size --- $93\%$ at $G=2$, $82\%$ at $G=4$,
$65\%$ at $G=8$, $46\%$ at $G=16$ --- and, because $\Sigma_b$ and $\Sigma_w$ are both divided by
$P$, entirely independent of the number of prompts. Adding prompts buys a smaller error bar; only
larger groups change what the error bar is made of.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/sources.pdf}
  \caption{The share of the forecast variance attributable to rollout sampling rather than prompt
  selection, against group size. Each point is the median over run lengths and prompt counts, which
  do not move it.}
  \label{fig:sources}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{No memory horizon.} If contraction were strong, the kernel $k_t$ of \eqref{eq:kernel}
would concentrate near the end of the run and reproducing a result would mean reproducing only its
tail. We looked for that and did not find it. Over $288$ settings, the number of final updates
holding $95\%$ of the forecast variance is a median of $1.00$ of the run, against $0.95$ for an
exactly uniform kernel, and it does not move when the step size is raised sixteen-fold or when the
run is started from a policy already trained for $1200$ updates. Filtering lowers the level of the
spread; over the horizons we can measure it does not localise where the spread was made. The right
reading is that reproducing a run means reproducing all of it.

\subsection{Why the forecast rests on filtering, not accumulation}
\label{sec:exp-seeds}

\paragraph{The recursion predicts divergence; a single timescale does not.} The enumerable policy
admits three things at once: the exact linearised recursion \eqref{eq:lyapunov}, the exact Fisher
information, and as many independent stochastic trajectories as we care to run. That allows
candidate models of seed divergence to be scored against ground truth rather than against each
other. Over twelve settings spanning prompt diversity, group size, batch size and step size, we
compare the propagated covariance against two alternatives: accumulation with no contraction, and
the single-timescale law an earlier version of this work relied on.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \small
  \input{tables/propagation}
  \caption{Predicted against measured divergence between independent runs in the enumerable policy,
  with $300$ trajectories per setting. Error is the ratio of predicted to measured, summarised as
  $\exp$ of the median absolute log ratio.}
  \label{tab:propagation}
\end{table}

The propagated recursion is accurate to $9\%$ at the median and $17\%$ at worst. Ignoring
contraction overshoots by up to $3.9\times$, and does so exactly where the policy is learning:
on the most homogeneous prompt pools, where the objective supplies the most curvature, it is wrong
by a factor of two and a half, while on heterogeneous pools where little learning happens it is
indistinguishable from the truth. The single-timescale law is wrong by two orders of magnitude.
That failure is informative rather than embarrassing: it is what \eqref{eq:unrolled} predicts when
a spectrum of contraction rates is compressed to one number.

\paragraph{Contraction requires a learning signal.} If divergence stops growing because the shared
objective pulls two runs together, then removing the objective while leaving the noise in place
should restore accumulation. We ran a population of transformers twice from identical parameters:
once normally, and once with every reward replaced by an independent coin flip, which leaves the
rollout sampling, the batch composition and the gradient magnitudes intact while destroying the
learning signal.

With a signal, divergence is flat while the pass rate climbs from $0.19$ to $0.51$. Without one it
grows by a factor of $10.7$ over the same eighty updates. The contraction is caused by the
objective, which is what \eqref{eq:seed-recursion} says and what separates this from a claim that
seeds happen to end up nearby.

\paragraph{On a pretrained model.} The transformers above are small and their task is synthetic.
We repeated the measurement on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with LoRA adapters, six runs differing only in
rollout randomness on exactly verifiable short-answer problems; because LoRA adapters start at zero
the runs begin from an identical policy by construction, and every checkpoint is scored on one fixed
batch of sequences.

The fifteen pairwise distances come from six runs and are not independent, so intervals are

apparent peak-and-fall is not resolved at six seeds. What the experiment supports is the weaker
statement that divergence does not accumulate on a pretrained model either. The six runs finish
within $0.835 \pm 0.044$ of each other in pass rate.

\paragraph{Bounded, not proven stationary.} Across the settings in which a run is still learning,
the log-log slope of divergence against update count runs from $-1.15$ to $+0.26$, against the
$+1$ that accumulation requires. That rejects accumulation. It does not establish stationarity in
the strict sense: $J_t$, $\Sigma_t$ and $\eta_t$ all move during training, so the level that
\eqref{eq:unrolled} converges to is itself slowly varying, and we describe the divergence as
bounded rather than stationary throughout.

\paragraph{How the level scales.} Compared at matched pass rate rather than matched step count,
a log-log fit over nine settings gives an exponent of $-0.61$ in the batch, $95\%$ CI
$[-1.42, +0.24]$, and $+0.59$ in the drift target, $95\%$ CI $[+0.27, +1.04]$, with $R^2 = 0.70$.
Both intervals contain the values $-1/2$ and $+1/2$ that follow from
$Q_t \propto \eta^2\Sigma/P$, and the batch interval also contains zero. Nine settings are too few
to pin a two-dimensional scaling law, and we report the intervals rather than the point estimates
for that reason.

\paragraph{Why the route through policy divergence was abandoned.} An earlier version of this work
predicted that the spread in final task performance would follow $\sqrt{\KL_\infty}$. It does not,
in our data: the fitted exponent is $+0.10$ with $95\%$ CI $[-0.03, +0.45]$ against a predicted
$+0.5$, and the interval contains zero. Policy divergence and outcome spread are positively
associated across our settings, but these experiments do not establish the predicted relation, and
we withdraw it.

The reason is structural rather than statistical, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-scale} shows it in its
extreme form. $\KL$ weights every direction the policy can move in by the Fisher information; a
task metric weights them by $\nabla M$, which is a single direction and can be nearly orthogonal to
where the variance is --- or, on a saturated evaluation, zero. No scalar function of $\KL$ can
recover that. Theorem~\ref{thm:adjoint} takes the direct route instead, and
Section~\ref{sec:exp-forecast} is what it delivers.

\subsection{How far the linearisation can be pushed}
\label{sec:exp-linearity}

Everything above linearises the mean update field between the two runs. That assumption is
checkable without knowing the answer, which makes it a diagnostic a run can apply to itself. For a
pair of runs at $\theta^A$ and $\theta^B$ with $\Delta = \theta^B - \theta^A$, define the residual
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:residual}
  \varepsilon \;:=\;
  \frac{\big\|\,\gbar(\theta^B) - \gbar(\theta^A) - J(\theta^A)\Delta\,\big\|}
       {\big\|\,\gbar(\theta^B) - \gbar(\theta^A)\,\big\|},
\end{equation}
zero where the field is linear between them. We measure its median along $36$ pairs of runs and
report the forecast error against it.

The residual behaves as it should: raising the step size eightfold raises it from $0.028$ to
$0.165$. The forecast does not follow. Its median error is $1.11\times$ where the residual is below
$5\%$, $1.18\times$ between $5$ and $12\%$, $1.08\times$ between $12$ and $25\%$, and $1.18\times$
above that, with a rank correlation of $0.17$ between residual and error across the grid. What does
track the residual is the tail: the worst case is $1.21\times$ below $5\%$ and around $1.57\times$
above it.

Two things follow. The derivation asks for more than the result needs --- a $16\%$ departure from
linearity costs nothing at the median --- so the honest statement of the assumption is that it
bounds the worst case rather than setting the accuracy. And $\varepsilon$ is worth logging anyway,
because it is the quantity that separates the regime where the forecast has a worst case of
$1.2\times$ from the regime where it has one of $1.6\times$, and computing it costs one extra
gradient evaluation on a checkpoint a run already has.

\subsection{Exactly enumerable policies}

The policy is a tabular autoregressive model over a small vocabulary, so its distribution over
sequences, its score at every sequence, and the conditional moments $(p, u_1, S_0, S_1)$ are
available exactly. Propositions~\ref{prop:mean} and~\ref{prop:cov} were checked against
brute-force enumeration over all $|\mathcal{Y}|^{G}$ group outcomes for five estimators and
$G \in \{2,3,4\}$: agreement to $10^{-12}$. The scaled within-prompt term $(G-1)\tr\Sw$, which the
theory treats as $G$-independent, varies by $6\%$ over $G = 2$ to $32$.

\paragraph{The curvature model fails, the drift model does not.} Taking a real update and computing
the resulting improvement and KL exactly, the second-order model of the objective places its optimal
step at least four times below the step that actually maximises improvement, which is where the
sweep ended, because $J = \E[r]$ is
not a log-likelihood and a log-linear policy keeps improving past the turnover of a quadratic model.
The drift model \eqref{eq:drift} predicts the measured KL to between $2.6\%$ and $3.9\%$ across a thousandfold range of step sizes. The theory in Section~\ref{sec:drift} is built on the second of these, not
the first.

\paragraph{Splitting a rollout budget.} For each prompt pool the parameter-free efficiency curve
$\rho(G)$ is compared against the improvement obtained by training at every split of a fixed budget,
holding total rollouts, total drift and step count fixed. Table~\ref{tab:exact-agreement} groups
pools by how much $\rho$ varies across the splits on offer.

\subsection{When the optimiser carries state}
\label{sec:exp-adam}

Adam's update depends on two moment estimates as well as the parameters, so a gradient perturbation
outlives the step that produced it and $\theta$ alone is no longer a closed state. The recursion
lifts: with $z = (\theta, m, v)$ and $\Phi_z$ the mean Adam map, the transfer operator is
$\partial\Phi_z/\partial z$ and the injection is $B\,\Sigma_t B^{\!\top}$ with
$B = \partial\Phi_z/\partial \hat{g}$, the sensitivity of one update to the gradient it was handed.
Both are taken by central differences on the exact mean map, so nothing about the derivation has to
be trusted.

One caveat is structural. Adam's map is singular at $v = 0$: the derivative of
$1/(\sqrt{v} + \epsilon)$ diverges there, and a recursion started at initialisation is not
linearisable at all. We warm the moments on mean gradients before propagating, which every run does
anyway, and every seed shares the warmed state so the covariance still starts at zero.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \small
  \input{tables/adam_lift}
  \caption{Predicted against measured seed divergence under Adam, over $24$ settings. The
  parameter-only row uses the same operators restricted to their $\theta$ block, so it isolates the
  cost of pretending the optimiser state does not carry a perturbation forward.}
  \label{tab:adam-lift}
\end{table}

The optimiser state matters and the lift is worth taking: ignoring it underpredicts by
$5.5\times$ at the median, and the lift brings that to $2.6\times$. Freezing the second moment
rather than propagating it makes matters worse, not better, which says the second moment is
genuinely carrying seed information rather than merely complicating the algebra.

But the lift is not reliable, and where it fails is systematic. On pools with a live learning
signal every setting lands within about a factor of two. On pools where the pass rate is saturated
at either end the linearised map has a spectral radius above one and the prediction runs away, by
up to two orders of magnitude in the worst case. This is Adam's normalisation acting as the
nonlinearity that bounds the real spread --- a bound a linear recursion does not contain. The
practical statement is that the forecast should be taken under gradient ascent, or under Adam only
while $\E_x[p(1-p)]$ is well away from zero, and that a linear treatment of Adam over long horizons
is an open problem rather than a solved one.

\subsection{A pretrained model}

We repeat the measurement on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with LoRA adapters as the trainable parameters,
over corpora of short exact-match tasks: counting a letter in a word, naming the $k$-th item of a
list, adding two numbers, and a mixture of all of them. Measured optimal group sizes run from $2.7$
to $6.2$, the same range as the enumerable policies and the transformers trained from scratch.

Corollary~\ref{cor:bernoulli} predicts that the within-prompt noise tracks the reward histogram at a
fixed score scale, and difficulty buckets inside one family are where that assumption is tenable.
We screen prompts with a larger sample than the measurement uses, so the bucket boundaries do not
inherit the noise of the quantity under test, and report the pass rate re-measured during the probe
rather than the one used to sort. Across buckets spanning pass rates from $0.25$ to $0.30$, the
ratio $\tau_w/\E[p(1-p)]$ is constant to within $14\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:difficulty}).

Across \emph{different} families the same ratio varies by a factor of $8.8$

predicts is the score scale $\sigma_s^2$, and a family whose answer is one number does not share a
score scale with one whose answer is four. This bounds how the corollary should be used. It
converts a reward histogram into a noise estimate within a task distribution, after one calibration
measurement, and it does not transport that calibration to a task of a different shape.

\subsection{At seven billion parameters, and a metric that cannot see}
\label{sec:exp-scale}

The largest model this machine holds is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at four-bit precision, trained with
LoRA adapters and AdamW on exactly verifiable short-answer problems. The first configuration we ran
produced a result we did not plan for and which is worth reporting before the one we did.

\paragraph{The reported number went blind.} On counting a letter in a word --- a task the base model
already passes at $0.41$ --- twenty updates take the held-out pass rate to $0.875$ and collapse the
response distribution. Every one of the $32$ held-out prompts then has a per-prompt rate of exactly
$0$ or exactly $1$: eight samples, one answer. Two runs differing only in rollout randomness report
\emph{the same number to the last decimal} and agree on every prompt individually, while their
policies differ by between $1.8\times10^{-5}$ and $7.7\times10^{-2}$ in KL and that difference does
not accumulate over the run.

This is worth being precise about, because the two readings are opposite. It is not that the seeds
converged. It is that the metric stopped resolving them: $\E_x[p(1-p)] = 0$ on the evaluation set,
so a score built from those samples has no variance to report, whatever the policies do. The
practical consequence is that a zero measured spread on a saturated benchmark is not evidence of a
reproducible procedure, and the theoretical consequence is the one
Section~\ref{sec:metric} is built on: the spread of a reported number is
$\nabla M^{\!\top} S_T \nabla M$, and when $\nabla M$ is zero on the evaluated distribution no amount
of $S_T$ reaches it. It is also, in retrospect, why the route through $\sqrt{\KL}$ that an earlier
version of this work took could not have worked in general.
