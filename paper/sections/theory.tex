\section{Supplying $\Sigma_t$: what the estimators compute, and how noisy they are}
\label{sec:theory}

Section~\ref{sec:seeds} left two objects abstract: the batch covariance $\Sigma_t$ that
\eqref{eq:lyapunov} injects, and the difficulty weight $\lambda$ that
Proposition~\ref{prop:potential} integrates into a potential. This section computes both, exactly,
at the group sizes actually used. Everything the forecast needs about the estimator is here.

All results in this section are exact at finite $G$, and all are verified against brute-force
enumeration in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. Define, for $m \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(G-1, p)$ and
$m' \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(G-2, p)$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:contractions}
    W_r &:= \E_m\big[w(r + m, r)\big], \notag \\
  Q_r &:= \E_m\big[w(r + m, r)^2\big], \notag \\
  C_{ab} &:= \E_{m'}\big[w(a + b + m', a)\, w(a + b + m', b)\big].
\end{align}

\subsection{The estimator zoo is a reweighting of prompt difficulty}

\begin{proposition}[Exact expectation]
  \label{prop:mean}
  For any estimator of the form \eqref{eq:family} and any binary reward,
    \begin{equation}
    \E[z \mid x] = \lambda(p, G)\, h(x), \quad \lambda := W_1(p,G) - W_0(p,G).
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}


Each of the following is exact for every $p$ and every $G$.
RLOO has $W_1 = 1-p$ and $W_0 = -p$, hence $\lambda \equiv 1$: it is unbiased.
GRPO with a mean baseline has $\lambda = (G-1)/G$, independent of $p$: it is RLOO multiplied by a
constant. Standardised GRPO has a $\lambda(p, G)$ with no closed form but an exact binomial sum.

So none of the estimators in Table~\ref{tab:family} points in the wrong direction \emph{on any
individual prompt}: each computes $h(x)$ scaled by a positive weight, for every $p \in (0,1)$ and
every $G$. That is a property of those weight tables and not of the family, since an arbitrary
$w(k,r)$ can plainly give $\lambda < 0$; the estimators in use happen to lie in the subset where
$W_1 > W_0$ throughout. This does not carry over to the aggregate. The family
optimises the reweighted field $\E_x[\lambda(p(x), G)\, h(x)]$, which need not be collinear with
$\E_x[h(x)]$ once prompts disagree, so a $p$-dependent $\lambda$ can rotate the direction the run
actually follows. Standardisation is therefore not \emph{merely} a variance-reduction transformation: under binary
rewards it also changes the expected gradient, upweighting prompts whose pass rate is near $0$ or
$1$ relative to prompts of intermediate difficulty. Whether it additionally reduces variance is a
separate question that Proposition~\ref{prop:mean} does not settle either way.

A constant $\lambda$ is invisible: rescaling an estimator by $c$ rescales the optimal step size by
$1/c$ and leaves every trajectory unchanged. Only the \emph{dependence of $\lambda$ on $p$} can
alter what a run converges to. This is the sense in which most of the zoo is one estimator.

\subsection{Exact covariance at finite group size}

\begin{proposition}[Exact per-prompt covariance]
  \label{prop:cov}
  With $U := u_1 u_1^{\!\top}$,
  \begin{equation}
    \Cov(z \mid x)
    = \frac{1}{G}\Big[ p\,Q_1 S_1 + (1-p)\,Q_0 S_0 \Big]
    + c_U(p, G)\, U ,
  \end{equation}
    \begin{align}
    c_U &= \frac{1}{G}\Big( p\,Q_1 + \frac{p^2 Q_0}{1-p} \Big) \notag \\
    &\quad + p^2\Big[ \tfrac{G-1}{G}\big( C_{11} - 2C_{10} + C_{00} \big) - \lambda^2 \Big].
  \end{align}
\end{proposition}


\subsection{Two levels of noise}

Prompts are drawn independently, so with $\gbar := \E_x[\lambda h]$ the batch estimator has
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:hierarchy}
  \Cov(\ghat) \;=\; \frac{1}{P}\Big[ \Sb + \Sw \Big],
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
  \Sb &:= \Cov_x\big( \lambda(p(x), G)\, h(x) \big), \notag \\
  \Sw &:= \E_x\big[ \Cov(z \mid x) \big]. \notag
\end{align}
$\Sb$ is the irreducible variance of sampling prompts and does not shrink with $G$; $\Sw$ is the
variance of sampling responses and does. Pretraining has one level of sampling and therefore one
noise scale. RLVR has two, and the split of a fixed rollout budget between them is a free choice
that is not determined by the noise scale alone. Concurrent work approaches the same split from a
different direction: \citet{zhou2026ustatistic} show the GRPO policy gradient is a U-statistic,
characterise its mean squared error at finite sample, and derive a scaling law for the group size
from that error. Our route is the two-level covariance itself, which is what lets the same
measurement serve the seed-uncertainty forecast and the allocation question together, and what
carries the prefill economics of Section~\ref{sec:applications}; the group-size result should be
read as one consequence of that decomposition rather than as the first principled treatment of
it.

\begin{corollary}[The reward histogram predicts the response-level noise]
  \label{cor:bernoulli}
  Exactly, from Proposition~\ref{prop:cov},
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sw-exact}
    \begin{aligned}
      \tr \Sw \;=\; \E_x\Big[
        &\tfrac{1}{G}\big( p Q_1 \tr S_1 + (1-p) Q_0 \tr S_0 \big) \\
        &+\; c_U \|u_1\|^2 \Big].
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  For RLOO, $p Q_1 + (1-p) Q_0 = p(1-p)\,G/(G-1)$ exactly, for every $G$. If in addition the
  conditional score covariances are comparable across the two reward classes,
  $\tr S_0 \approx \tr S_1 \approx \sigma_s^2$, and the outer-product term is small against the
  first, then
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bernoulli}
    \tr \Sw \;\approx\; \frac{\sigma_s^2}{G-1}\, \E_x\big[ p(x)\,(1 - p(x)) \big].
  \end{equation}
\end{corollary}

The response-level noise is then, up to one slowly varying scalar, the mean Bernoulli variance of
the pass-rate distribution: a quantity every RLVR trainer already logs, at no cost.

Both conditions are approximations rather than identities, and we measure both rather than assert
them. The outer-product term dropped in \eqref{eq:bernoulli} carries between $5.6\%$ and $9.6\%$ of
$\tr\Sw$ over the pools and group sizes used here, falling as $G$ grows, so \eqref{eq:bernoulli}
runs that much low; the exact form \eqref{eq:sw-exact} is what the implementation evaluates and what
every number in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} uses. The comparability of $\tr S_0$ and $\tr S_1$ is
a statement about conditional score scales, not about the shape of either covariance, and
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports how far it holds across difficulty buckets.
