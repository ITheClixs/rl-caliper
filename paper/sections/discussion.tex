\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What this establishes.} That training noise in RLVR is filtered rather than accumulated,
that the filter is the shared objective and not an artefact of runs starting nearby, and that the
covariance recursion \eqref{eq:lyapunov} predicts the resulting divergence to within about $10\%$
where it can be checked exactly. The practical readings are that run length does not enter the
answer, and that batch composition does, with square-root returns.

\paragraph{What it does not establish.} We do not deliver an error bar on a benchmark number from a
single run. Three things stand between here and there. The recursion predicts \emph{policy}
divergence, and our attempt to carry that to \emph{outcome} spread through $\sqrt{\KL}$ failed; the
right route is to propagate the covariance against the gradient of the target metric directly,
which \eqref{eq:unrolled} supports and we have not implemented. The transfer operators $A_t$ are
computed exactly only in the enumerable policy; on a real model they would need Jacobian-vector
products along a stored trajectory. And the largest model we measure is $0.5$B, with six seeds over
forty updates on short-answer tasks.

\paragraph{On the size of the models.} The obvious next experiment is a 3--7B reasoning model with
a dozen seeds over several hundred updates, measuring whether the same recursion predicts the same
quantities there. We did not run it, and not because it was overlooked: every experiment in this
paper was executed on a single laptop, which is enough for exact validation on solvable systems and
for populations of small transformers, and is not enough for that. We would rather state the
boundary than gesture past it.

\paragraph{Against drift targeting, in one case.} Section~\ref{sec:drift} recommends specifying a
run by a drift target rather than a learning rate, and Section~\ref{sec:experiments} shows that
target transferring across batch size where a learning rate does not. The same technique destroys
two of our runs. The mechanism is specific: $\E_x[p(1-p)] \to 0$ as a task is solved, the signal
goes with it, and a controller holding drift constant answers by raising the step without bound.
Any drift-targeted schedule needs a floor on the signal or a cap on the step; ours had neither. The
defensible claim is that behavioural drift is a more invariant coordinate for update magnitude, not
that a constant value of it is a good schedule.

\paragraph{Limits of the linearisation.} \eqref{eq:seed-recursion} requires the two runs to stay
close enough that the mean gradient field is locally linear between them, and
$\eta\|J_t\| \ll 1$. Both fail at saturation, which is the same boundary the drift controller
crosses. A diagnostic exists and is cheap: the realised drift departing from its target is the
observable that announces it.

\paragraph{What we would do next, in order.} Propagate the covariance against a task metric rather
than the Fisher information, which converts a statement about policies into an error bar on a
reported number. Implement the transfer operators through Jacobian-vector products so the recursion
runs on a real model without materialising anything. Then measure the per-update kernel on a run
long enough for the question to be interesting, and ask how much of the final variance was
generated in the last few percent of updates, because if that fraction is large then replicating a
run means replicating only its tail.
