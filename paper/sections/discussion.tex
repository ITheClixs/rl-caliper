\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What this establishes.} That the across-seed spread of a reported RLVR score can be
forecast from a single training run, to a median factor of $1.08$ over $48$ settings where the
comparison can be made exactly; that the mechanism is filtering rather than accumulation, and that
the filter is the shared objective and not an artefact of runs starting nearby; and that the
backward pass the forecast needs is cheap because the mean update of a count-based estimator is a
gradient field. The practical readings are that run length does not enter the answer, that batch
composition does with square-root returns, and that rollout sampling rather than prompt selection
is what the reported number mostly remembers.

\paragraph{One quantity runs through all of it.} Read together, the measurements in this paper
keep returning to $\E_x[p(1-p)]$, the mean within-prompt reward variance. It sets $\Sigma_t$, so it
sets how much seed noise a batch injects. It sets the learning signal, so it sets how much of that
noise the following updates filter. It is largest before a policy has learned anything, which is
why the per-update kernel tilts towards the start of a run. And when it reaches zero the injection
stops, the metric stops resolving the policy, and the linearisation of Adam's normalisation stops
holding --- three of the four boundaries below are the same boundary. A practitioner who logs one
number from this paper should log that one.

\paragraph{What ``filtered'' does and does not mean.} It would be tidier if the transfer operator
were a contraction. It is not: its spectral radius exceeds one throughout a run, only a third to a
half of directions contract, and the contracting timescales differ among themselves by more than an
order of magnitude. Divergence stays bounded over the horizons anyone trains because expansion is
slow relative to run length --- a factor of about $1.6$ over twenty-five updates --- and not because
perturbations are destroyed. A run long enough would leave this regime, and we cannot say from here
how long that is. The recursion is indifferent to the distinction, which is the argument for
carrying the operator rather than a summary of it; the prose is not, which is why we say filtered
and not contracted.

\paragraph{On parameter space.} $S_T$ lives in parameter space, and a covariance there is not
obviously about anything a reader of a paper cares about --- a reparameterisation could inflate or
shrink it without changing the policy. That objection is answered by what the forecast predicts
rather than by an argument. \eqref{eq:adjoint} contracts $S_T$ against $\nabla M$, so what is being
tested is a functional of the policy: the standard deviation of a pass rate, in the units the pass
rate is reported in. A quantity that were merely a coordinate artefact could not predict that to
$\numForecastMedian\times$ over $\numForecastSettings$ settings. The same contraction is why
policy divergence and metric spread come apart in Section~\ref{sec:exp-scale}: they weight the
directions of $S_T$ differently, and one of the two weightings can be identically zero.

\paragraph{What it does not establish.} The forecast is validated where the metric, its gradient
and the injected covariance are all exact. On a real model each becomes an estimate, and the
composition of three estimates is not something our measurements bound. One of the three is biased
in a direction we can name. Writing the estimated metric gradient as $\hat{b} = \nabla M + \epsilon$
with $\epsilon$ the sampling error of one rollout on the evaluation set,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bias}
  \E\big[\Var(\hat{b}^{\!\top}\hat{g})\big]
  = \Var(\nabla M^{\!\top}\hat{g}) + \E\big[\Var(\epsilon^{\!\top}\hat{g})\big],
\end{equation}
so a noisy metric gradient makes the forecast too large, never too small. The second term shrinks
with the size of the evaluation rollout and can be estimated by splitting it, which we did not do. We report the ingredients
separately at $7$B --- the realised spread over eight seeds, and the divergence between them --- but
not the forecast itself, because we could not validate the backward pass at that scale within this
machine's budget. That gap is the single most valuable thing to close next, and
Section~\ref{sec:exp-forecast} states exactly what it would take: two gradient evaluations per
stored update, with common random numbers so the difference is not swamped.

\paragraph{On the size of the models.} The largest measurement here is Qwen2.5-7B with eight seeds
over twenty updates. That is enough to say that a $7$B model's reported number moves under
reseeding by an amount we can measure and that its policy divergence does not accumulate; it is not
enough to say anything about a run of several hundred updates on a reasoning model. We did not run
that, and not because it was overlooked: every experiment in this paper was executed on a single
laptop, which is enough for exact validation on solvable systems, for populations of small
transformers, and for eight seeds at $7$B, and is not enough for that. We would rather state the
boundary than gesture past it.

\paragraph{Two zeros that mean different things.} The most immediately usable thing in this paper
may be a warning rather than a method. A run can report a zero seed spread for two reasons that
look identical from outside. The response distribution can collapse, in which case
$\Sigma_t = 0$ and no seed uncertainty is generated after that point; or the evaluation can
saturate, in which case $\nabla M = 0$ on the evaluated distribution and no seed uncertainty is
detected. Section~\ref{sec:exp-scale} exhibits both on real models, the first within a single update
at a step size a practitioner might well pick. Neither is evidence that the training procedure is
reproducible, and both are cheap to rule out: log the fraction of prompts whose group is not
unanimous during training, and $\E_x[p(1-p)]$ on the evaluation set at the end. If either is zero,
so is the error bar, and it means nothing.

\paragraph{Adam.} Lifting the recursion to $z = (\theta, m, v)$ is the right move in principle and
only partly works in practice. It halves the error of ignoring the optimiser state --- $2.6\times$ against $5.5\times$ ---
and the optimiser state clearly does carry seed information, since freezing the second moment takes
the error to $15.9\times$. Where the learning signal is live the lift is good to $1.48\times$ at
the median. Where the pass rate saturates at either end, Adam's normalisation is the nonlinearity
that bounds the spread, and a linear recursion cannot see a bound it does not contain. Section~\ref{sec:exp-adam} reports where it holds and where it does not, rather than
reporting the median alone.

\paragraph{Limits of the linearisation, with a diagnostic.} \eqref{eq:seed-recursion} requires the
two runs to stay close enough that the mean gradient field is locally linear between them, and
$\eta\|\mathcal{J}_t\| \ll 1$. Section~\ref{sec:exp-linearity} measures the residual
$\|\gbar(\theta^B) - \gbar(\theta^A) - \mathcal{J}(\theta^A)\Delta\| / \|\gbar(\theta^B) - \gbar(\theta^A)\|$
along real pairs of runs and reports the forecast error against it. The residual is computable
during training without knowing the answer, which is what makes it useful: a run can say whether
its own forecast is inside the regime that produced it.

\paragraph{Against drift targeting, in one case.} Section~\ref{sec:drift} recommends specifying a
run by a drift target rather than a learning rate, and Section~\ref{sec:applications} shows that
target transferring across batch size where a learning rate does not. The same technique destroys
two of our runs. The mechanism is specific: $\E_x[p(1-p)] \to 0$ as a task is solved, the signal
goes with it, and a controller holding drift constant answers by raising the step without bound.
Any drift-targeted schedule needs a floor on the signal or a cap on the step; ours had neither. The
defensible claim is that behavioural drift is a more invariant coordinate for update magnitude, not
that a constant value of it is a good schedule.

\paragraph{What we would do next, in order.} Validate the backward pass on a real model, which is
an engineering task rather than a scientific one and which turns Section~\ref{sec:exp-forecast}
into a tool. Then ask whether the forecast survives a run long enough for the kernel to have
structure, since over the horizons reachable here it does not. Then treat the estimation error in
$\nabla M$ and $\Sigma_t$ properly, because on a real model the error bar on the error bar is the
thing a practitioner will actually be handed.
