\section{From one run to the spread of its result}
\label{sec:seeds}

Fix a policy $\theta_0$ and start two runs from it. They see the same prompts and the same
objective, and differ only in the randomness of their rollouts. This is the comparison a
practitioner makes when they change a seed and rerun, and the question this section answers is how
far apart the numbers they report end up, using only one of the runs.

\subsection{Noise is filtered, not accumulated}

Write $\Delta_t := \theta^A_t - \theta^B_t$. With updates $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta\ghat$
and $\mathcal{J}_t := \partial\gbar/\partial\theta$ the Jacobian of the mean update,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:seed-recursion}
  \Delta_{t+1} = A_t \Delta_t + \eta\,\nu_t,
  \qquad A_t := I + \eta \mathcal{J}_t,
\end{equation}
to first order in $\Delta$, with $\Cov(\nu_t) = 2\Sigma_t/P$. The batch noise $\Sigma_t$ is left abstract here and made
explicit in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, where it is computed exactly at finite group size; nothing
in this section depends on which estimator produced it.

If the objective were flat, $\mathcal{J} = 0$, this would be a random walk: divergence would
accumulate, $\E\|\Delta_t\|^2 \propto t$, and a longer run would be strictly less reproducible than
a short one. That is the implicit model behind treating run-to-run variance as something to be
measured by brute force. But two RLVR runs are climbing the same objective, and $A_t$ reweights a
perturbation by the local curvature rather than passing it through. We will call this filtering
rather than contraction, because Section~\ref{sec:exp-spectrum} measures the spectrum of $A_t$ and
finds it straddling one: a minority of directions contract, the leading direction expands slowly,
and the contracting timescales differ among themselves by more than an order of magnitude.

The consequence is that noise from update $t$ is not added to the final divergence. It is passed
through every subsequent update first.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-time covariance]
  \label{thm:propagation}
  Let $S_t$ denote the covariance of a run about its mean trajectory, with $S_0 = 0$ when both runs
  start from the same policy. Under the linearisation \eqref{eq:seed-recursion},
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lyapunov}
    S_{t+1} = A_t S_t A_t^{\!\top} + Q_t,
    \qquad Q_t := \eta^2 \Sigma_t / P,
  \end{equation}
  and therefore
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:unrolled}
    S_T = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \Phi_{T,t+1}\, Q_t\, \Phi_{T,t+1}^{\!\top},
    \qquad \Phi_{T,s} := \!\!\prod_{u=s}^{T-1}\!\! A_u ,
  \end{equation}
  the product taken in order of decreasing $u$.
  Two independent seeds satisfy $\Cov(\theta^A_T - \theta^B_T) = 2S_T$, so their expected policy
  divergence is
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:predicted-kl}
    \E\big[\KL(\pi^A \,\|\, \pi^B)\big] \;\approx\; \tr\!\big(F_T\, S_T\big).
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Taking the covariance of \eqref{eq:seed-recursion} for a single run about its mean leaves a
  cross term $\E[\Delta_t \nu_t^{\!\top}]$. It vanishes without assuming $\nu_t$ and $\Delta_t$ are
  independent, which they are not: the batch noise is drawn at $\theta_t$, so its law depends on a
  parameter that earlier noise produced. What holds is that it is a martingale difference with
  respect to the filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$ generated by the run up to $t$,
  $\E[\nu_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0$, and $\Delta_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable, so
  $\E[\Delta_t\nu_t^{\!\top}] = \E\big[\Delta_t\,\E[\nu_t^{\!\top}\mid\mathcal{F}_t]\big] = 0$.
  That gives \eqref{eq:lyapunov}; unrolling it from $S_0 = 0$ gives \eqref{eq:unrolled}. Two independent runs have independent noise, so the covariance of
  their difference is twice that of one about the mean. Expanding
  $\KL(\pi^A\|\pi^B) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^{\!\top}F\Delta$ and taking expectations gives
  $\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(F\cdot 2S_T)$.
\end{proof}

Equation \eqref{eq:unrolled} is the object this section is about, and it is worth reading as a
convolution: what survives to the end is the noise injected at each update, weighted by how
strongly the remaining trajectory preserves a perturbation entering there. Write
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kernel}
  k_t \;:=\; \tr\!\big(F_T\, \Phi_{T,t+1} Q_t \Phi_{T,t+1}^{\!\top}\big),
  \qquad
  \E[\KL] = \sum_{t} k_t,
\end{equation}
for the contribution of update $t$. Two runs are not equally sensitive to noise at every point in
training, and $k_t$ says where the sensitivity is.

\subsection{Why a single timescale is not enough}

An earlier version of this work replaced the spectrum of $A_t$ by one effective eigenvalue,
giving $\E[\KL_\infty] = \tau_c D_{\mathrm{noise}}$ for a persistence time $\tau_c$. That
approximation is the natural first move and it fails badly:
Section~\ref{sec:exp-seeds} measures it at a median error of two orders of magnitude, against
$\numPropagatedPercent\%$ for \eqref{eq:unrolled}. The reason is visible in \eqref{eq:unrolled}. $A_t$ has a spectrum,
fast directions equilibrate within a few updates while slow ones set the final level, and a single
fitted exponential is pinned by the early rise and blind to what dominates the answer. We report
the scalar law only as the comparator it turned out to be.

\subsection{What the recursion predicts}

\paragraph{Divergence stops growing when, and because, the run is learning.} $\mathcal{J}_t$ is
the curvature of the estimator potential $\Psi$, and it is what makes $A_t$ anything other than the
identity. With no learning signal there is no potential to be curved: $\mathcal{J}_t \to 0$,
$A_t \to I$, and \eqref{eq:seed-recursion} degenerates to a random walk in which every update's
noise survives to the end unaltered. Note that this does not require $\mathcal{J}_t$ to be negative
definite, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-spectrum} finds that it is not; what it requires is that the
directions carrying the variance are ones the objective bends. The claim is testable directly by
removing the signal while leaving the noise untouched, which Section~\ref{sec:exp-seeds} does.

\paragraph{The level is set by batch composition.} $Q_t \propto \Sigma_t/P$, so halving the
injected noise requires quadrupling the batch, and nothing in \eqref{eq:unrolled} depends on the
number of updates once the sum has converged.

\paragraph{A memory horizon, if the filter is sharp enough to make one.} If $\Phi_{T,t+1}$
decays with $T - t$, the sum in \eqref{eq:unrolled} is dominated by its last terms and the final
$h \ll T$ updates account for nearly all of the spread; reproducing a result would then mean
reproducing its end. Where the filter is close to the identity, $k_t$ is flat and every update
counts the same.
Section~\ref{sec:exp-seeds} asks which of these holds and finds the second, over every setting we
can reach.

\subsection{Carrying it to the number that gets reported}
\label{sec:metric}

Equation \eqref{eq:predicted-kl} is a statement about policies. What a paper reports is a score:
pass rate, exact match, win rate, mean reward. Write $M(\theta)$ for that score as a function of
the parameters. Linearising it about the endpoint the run reached,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:metric-variance}
  \Var_{\text{seed}}\big[M(\theta_T)\big] \;\approx\; \nabla M(\theta_T)^{\!\top} S_T\, \nabla M(\theta_T),
\end{equation}
which needs $S_T$, a matrix nobody can form for a real model. It is not needed. Substituting
\eqref{eq:unrolled} turns the quadratic form into a sum of scalars.

\begin{theorem}[Adjoint form of the reported spread]
  \label{thm:adjoint}
  Define $b_T := \nabla M(\theta_T)$ and $b_s := A_s^{\!\top} b_{s+1}$ for $s = T-1, \dots, 0$.
  Then, under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:propagation},
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:adjoint}
    \begin{aligned}
      \Var_{\text{seed}}\big[M(\theta_T)\big]
      &\;=\; \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} b_{t+1}^{\!\top} Q_t\, b_{t+1} \\
      &\;=\; \frac{\eta^2}{P}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \Var_{i}\!\big(b_{t+1}^{\!\top} z_{t,i}\big),
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  where $z_{t,i}$ is the per-prompt estimator of Section~\ref{sec:setup} at $\theta_t$ and the
  variance is taken over prompts. Since $\ghat_t = P^{-1}\sum_i z_{t,i}$, the same term is
  $\eta^2\Var(b_{t+1}^{\!\top}\ghat_t)$; the factor $P$ belongs with the per-prompt form and not
  with the batch one, and we use the per-prompt form throughout because it is what
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:forecast} accumulates.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  $b_s = \Phi_{T,s}^{\!\top}\nabla M$ by induction. Substituting \eqref{eq:unrolled} into
  \eqref{eq:metric-variance} gives $\sum_t (\Phi_{T,t+1}^{\!\top}\nabla M)^{\!\top} Q_t
  (\Phi_{T,t+1}^{\!\top}\nabla M)$. The second equality is $Q_t = \eta^2\Sigma_t/P$ together with
  $b^{\!\top}\Sigma_t b = \Var_i(b^{\!\top} z_{t,i})$, since $\Sigma_t = \Sb + \Sw$ is the
  covariance of one prompt's contribution rather than of the batch mean.
\end{proof}

Every term of \eqref{eq:adjoint} is the variance of a batch gradient projected onto a single
direction: one scalar per update, which a trainer can accumulate alongside the update it is
already making. What the backward pass costs depends on $A_s^{\!\top}$, and here the estimator
family pays for itself.

\begin{proposition}[The mean update is a gradient field]
  \label{prop:potential}
  For any count-based estimator, $\gbar(\theta) = \nabla_\theta\,\Psi(\theta)$ with
  $\Psi(\theta) = \E_x[\Lambda(p_x)]$ and $\Lambda' = \lambda$. Hence $\mathcal{J} = \nabla^2\Psi$ is
  symmetric and $A_s^{\!\top} = A_s$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  By Proposition~\ref{prop:mean}, proved in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, the per-prompt mean is
  $\lambda(p_x, G)\,\nabla_\theta p_x$, and
  $\lambda$ depends on $\theta$ only through $p_x$. So
  $\lambda(p_x)\nabla p_x = \nabla \Lambda(p_x)$, and averaging over $x$ commutes with the
  gradient.
\end{proof}

We call $\Psi$ the \emph{estimator potential}. It is not the objective $J$ of
Section~\ref{sec:setup} unless $\lambda \equiv 1$; the gap between them is exactly the difficulty
reweighting that Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes explicit. The backward pass is therefore a
Hessian-vector product, which finite differences deliver from two gradient evaluations and reverse
mode from one double backward. Neither $\mathcal{J}$ nor $S_T$ is ever
formed. For RLOO, where $\lambda \equiv 1$, the potential is the pass rate itself: the mean update
ascends exactly the quantity being reported.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{The forecast, as a trainer would run it}
\label{alg:forecast}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State train as usual, storing $\theta_t$ and $\eta_t$ for each update
  \State $b \gets \nabla M(\theta_T)$ \Comment{one rollout and backward pass on the eval set}
  \State $v \gets 0$
  \For{$t = T-1$ \textbf{down to} $0$}
    \State draw a batch at $\theta_t$; let $\hat{g}_{t,i}$ be its per-prompt gradients
    \State $v \gets v + (\eta_t^2/P)\,\widehat{\Var}_i\big(b^{\!\top}\hat{g}_{t,i}\big)$
      \Comment{one scalar}
    \If{transport is needed}
      \State $\delta \gets \epsilon\, b/\|b\|$
      \State $b \gets b + \dfrac{\eta_t}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\|b\|}{\epsilon}
        \big(\gbar(\theta_t + \delta) - \gbar(\theta_t - \delta)\big)$
        \Comment{common random numbers}
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\sqrt{v}$ \Comment{forecast s.d.\ of $M$ across seeds; $\sqrt{2v}$ is the s.d.\ of
  the signed difference between two runs}
\end{algorithmic}
\vspace{2pt}
{\footnotesize Lines 7--10 are what the recursion asks for and what
Section~\ref{sec:exp-forecast} finds costs three times as much for a $2\%$ correction over runs of
this length. Omitting them gives \eqref{eq:cheap}.}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{What a practitioner would do with this.} Three things during the run, none of which
changes the run: store $\theta_t$ and $\eta_t$ at each update, accumulate the scalar
$\widehat{\Var}_i(b^{\!\top}\hat{g}_{t,i})$ once $b$ is known, and log the fraction of prompts whose
group is not unanimous. Then one rollout and backward pass on the evaluation set for
$\nabla M(\theta_T)$, and the backward sweep of Algorithm~\ref{alg:forecast}. The extra cost is two
gradient evaluations per stored update and one vector of parameter size held in memory.

And one thing before believing the answer: check that the runs it describes are small perturbations
of each other. The pairwise residual of Section~\ref{sec:exp-linearity} measures that directly on
a pair of runs already in hand, and
Section~\ref{sec:exp-real} is what happens when nobody checks, on a real run whose seeds separate
into groups, where the forecast is short by a factor of twenty-five and no amount of care in
computing it would have helped.

\paragraph{What it costs, plainly.} As written, the backward pass makes three passes per stored
update: one batch for the injected term, and two for the Hessian-vector product. That is about
three times the compute of the training run itself, not a rounding error, and we would rather state
it than let a reader discover it.

It is also usually unnecessary. Section~\ref{sec:exp-forecast} ablates the transport, holding
$b$ at $\nabla M(\theta_T)$ instead of carrying it back, and finds the forecast changes by a
median of $2\%$ and the median error not at all, over runs of ten and twenty-five updates. The
reason is in the spectrum: most of $A_t$ sits within $10^{-4}$ of one and $\nabla M$ points into
that part of it, so $\Phi_{T,t}$ is close to the identity along the one direction the forecast
needs. What we recommend, then, is the cheap estimator,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cheap}
  \Var_{\text{seed}}\big[M(\theta_T)\big] \;\approx\;
  \sum_{t} \frac{\eta_t^2}{P}\,\widehat{\Var}_i\!\big(\nabla M(\theta_T)^{\!\top}\hat{g}_{t,i}\big),
\end{equation}
one extra pass per update and no Hessian-vector products, with the full recursion held in
reserve for runs long enough, or curvature strong enough, that a per-update discrepancy of a percent
compounds. Knowing which regime one is in is what the recursion is for, and the spectrum of $A_t$ is
what says it.

Equation \eqref{eq:cheap} is \eqref{eq:adjoint} with $\Phi_{T,t} \approx I$ along $\nabla M$; it is
not a different method but the same one, evaluated where the operator is measured to be close to
the identity.

The comparison that matters is not against zero. Estimating the same quantity by replication costs
$N$ full runs and returns an $N$-seed estimate whose own interval is wide. At six seeds it is wide
enough to leave the shape of a divergence trace unresolved (Section~\ref{sec:exp-seeds}). One extra
pass per update is about the cost of a second run, and it is available on a run that has already
finished.

The number that comes out is the seed component only. A reported score also carries the randomness
of the evaluation set, which is the binomial term the eval literature already estimates
\citep{miller2024adding}. The two are independent by construction, one being over training seeds at
a fixed evaluation and the other over evaluation draws at a fixed model, so they add in quadrature,
and the practical recommendation is to report both terms rather than either alone. On a saturated
benchmark the second is small and the first is invisible, which is exactly the situation
Section~\ref{sec:exp-scale} runs into.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. First, this is a \emph{forecast}: \eqref{eq:adjoint} reads
only the states one run visited, so it can be computed before any second run exists, which is how
Section~\ref{sec:exp-seeds} tests it. Second, restricting $\Sigma_t$ to one of its two terms
attributes the answer by origin, since $\Sigma_b$ is which prompts were drawn and $\Sigma_w$ is
which responses were sampled. The two shares are exact and sum to one.

\subsection{Where the argument stops}

Everything above linearises the update around the shared trajectory, which requires the two runs to
stay close enough for $\gbar$ to be locally linear between them, and requires
$\eta\|\mathcal{J}_t\| \ll 1$. Both fail when the step grows relative to the curvature, and RLVR supplies a
concrete way for that to happen: a controller holding drift at a fixed target must raise $\eta$ as
the signal decays, and $\E_x[p(1-p)] \to 0$ as a task is solved takes the signal with it.
Section~\ref{sec:exp-seeds} shows runs crossing out of the regime exactly this way.

A second boundary is not a failure of the linearisation but a property of the run, and the kernel
makes it visible. A count-based advantage is identically zero on a group whose responses all earn
the same reward, so once a policy has become deterministic on the corpus, $\Sigma_t = 0$ and
\emph{no noise enters at all}. The forecast then reports that every later update contributes
nothing, which is correct: two seeds from that point on are the same run. Whether this is good news
depends on why the policy stopped sampling. Section~\ref{sec:exp-scale} shows an RLVR run reaching
it in a single update at a step size a practitioner might well choose, and the diagnostic that
separates it from an estimation failure is the fraction of prompts whose group is not unanimous,
which costs nothing to log.
