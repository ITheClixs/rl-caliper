\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards is now the standard way to turn a pretrained
model into a reasoning one \citep{deepseekr1}, and a paper reports that it moved a benchmark by two
points. Whether
that is a result depends on a number nobody has: how much the same recipe would have moved it with
a different seed. The problem is documented \citep{henderson2018matters, hochlehnert2025sober} and
the only instrument the field has is replication, which at current RL compute is close to
unaffordable. So the number usually goes unmeasured, and single-run improvements are read as
though the training procedure were deterministic.

This paper asks what a single run can be made to say about that number. The answer has two halves.
Where the comparison can be made exactly, a run does contain enough information to forecast its own
spread, and we derive the estimator that extracts it and test it prospectively. Carried to a
pretrained model the recommended estimator underpredicts by a factor of twenty-five and the
transported one by sixteen, for a reason the same framework identifies:
the runs stop being small perturbations of each other. Both halves are reported, because a method
that is presented without its boundary is not a method.

Getting there means first correcting the model of where the spread comes from. The obvious picture
is that gradient noise perturbs each update, perturbations accumulate, two runs execute a random
walk away from each other, and reproducibility decays with run length. That picture is implicit in
how run-to-run variance is discussed, and it is what a noisy gradient suggests. It is wrong. Noise
from one update does not survive to the end of training unchanged: it is passed through every
update that follows, which reweights it. What reaches the end is a convolution of injected noise
with the sensitivity of the remaining trajectory, an object that obeys a recursion and that can
be carried against the gradient of the reported metric instead of being formed.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An error bar from one run} (Theorem~\ref{thm:adjoint} and
  Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}). Carrying the metric gradient backwards along a single stored
  trajectory gives the across-seed variance of the reported score as a sum of scalars, one per
  update, with no covariance matrix formed. The transpose the backward pass would need is free,
  because the mean update of any count-based estimator is the gradient of a scalar potential
  $\E_x[\Lambda(p_x)]$ and its Jacobian is therefore symmetric. For RLOO the potential is the
  pass rate itself. In its cheap form, which an ablation shows loses nothing at these run lengths,
  it costs one extra pass per update, against $N$ full runs for replication. We test it
  prospectively, training one run and freezing it and recording the forecast before the remaining
  seeds were trained. Its median absolute error against the realised spread is
  $\numForecastMedian\times$ across $\numForecastSettings$ settings spanning two policy shapes, two
  prompt-pool diversities and a $\numForecastSpreadRange\times$ range of spreads. Its worst is
  $\numForecastWorst\times$, and it falls inside the measured $95\%$ interval in
  $\numForecastInside$ of them.

  \item \textbf{A finite-time covariance law, and what its operator really does}
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:propagation}, Section~\ref{sec:exp-spectrum}).
  $S_{t+1} = A_t S_t A_t^{\!\top} + Q_t$ predicts measured seed divergence to
  $\numPropagatedPercent\%$ at the median over $\numPropagationSettings$ settings, against
  $\numWalkWorst\times$ at worst for accumulation and two orders of magnitude for a
  single-timescale approximation. Whether the filter matters is itself a measurement: accumulation
  is wrong by a factor of two on homogeneous prompt pools and by three percent on heterogeneous
  ones, which is what the recursion predicts, since a pool of similar prompts is where the
  objective's curvature is consistent. Measuring the spectrum of $A_t$ says why: it is not a contraction.
  The spectral radius exceeds one throughout, by up to $2\%$ early and decaying as the policy
  improves; only $\numSpectrumShareLow$--$\numSpectrumShareHigh\%$ of directions contract, a share that grows during the run; and the
  contracting timescales span tens of updates to effectively unbounded. Divergence stays bounded
  because expansion is slow relative to run length, not because every direction shrinks.

  \item \textbf{An honest accounting of what the backward pass buys.} Holding the adjoint at
  $\nabla M$ instead of carrying it back changes the forecast by a median of $2\%$ and the median
  error not at all, so the Hessian-vector products, two of the three passes per update, are
  unnecessary over runs of this length. The recursion explains why: most of the spectrum of $A_t$
  sits within $10^{-4}$ of one and $\nabla M$ points into that part of it. We recommend the cheap
  estimator and say what would make the full one necessary.

  \item \textbf{The filtering is caused by learning.} Replacing rewards with coin flips, which
  leaves the rollouts, the batch composition and the step sizes untouched, turns a divergence trace
  that ends at $\numNullLearning$ of where it started into one that grows by a factor of $\numNullCoin$ over eighty
  updates, a gap of $\numNullGap\times$ at the end of the run. The learning condition also moves
  \emph{further} per update, so what brings those runs back together is not smaller steps.

  \item \textbf{Where the uncertainty comes from.} Restricting the injected covariance to one of
  its terms attributes the forecast by origin. The share owed to which responses were sampled
  rather than which prompts were drawn falls from $93\%$ at $G=2$ to $46\%$ at $G=16$, so the
  majority changes hands over the range we measure; with prompts drawn independently the number of
  prompts scales both terms equally and does not move it.

  \item \textbf{On a real model it underpredicts, and we say why} (Section~\ref{sec:exp-real}).
  Run end to end on Qwen2.5-0.5B, the recommended cheap estimator is a factor of twenty-five short
  of the measured spread and the transported one sixteen, because the eight runs end in groups rather than scattered around one and a linearised
  model has no way to represent that. What survives is narrower than the magnitude: the share of
  prompts whose group is not unanimous falls from about a half to under $5\%$ by the fifth update,
  which is measured rather than modelled, so the run stops injecting seed noise there. The failed
  forecast's own kernel puts $\numRealFirstHalfShare\%$ of its predicted variance before that
  point, but whether the actual seed variance is distributed the same way we did not test.


  \item \textbf{A metric can go blind before the seeds agree.} At $7$B on a task the base model
  already mostly passes, twenty updates collapse the response distribution: every held-out prompt
  scores exactly $0$ or $1$, two seeds report the same number to the last decimal, and their
  policies still differ by up to $8\times10^{-2}$ in KL. A zero measured spread on a saturated
  benchmark is not evidence of a reproducible procedure.

  \item \textbf{The machinery that makes the injected noise measurable}: an exact finite-$G$ mean
  and covariance for the count-based advantage estimators under binary rewards
  (Propositions~\ref{prop:mean} and \ref{prop:cov}), a decomposition of the gradient noise into
  between-prompt and within-prompt terms, and an estimator that recovers both from the microbatch
  gradients a trainer already accumulates. The same decomposition fixes the compute-optimal group
  size (Theorem~\ref{thm:split}), which Section~\ref{sec:applications} develops as an application.

  \item \textbf{An explicit account of what does not hold.} A single effective timescale fails by
  two orders of magnitude. A predicted square-root relation between policy divergence and outcome
  spread is not supported by our data and is withdrawn. We looked for a short memory horizon, a
  long run whose spread is generated only near its end, and found the opposite: in
  $\numTiltedEarly$ of $\numMemorySettings$ settings more of the final variance was injected in the
  first half of the run than the second, because the injected covariance is largest before the
  policy has learned anything. Lifting the
  recursion to Adam's optimiser state improves on ignoring that state by a factor of two at the
  median but is unreliable where the pass rate saturates. The largest model we measure is $7$B with
  eight seeds, which bounds what any of this establishes about frontier-scale RLVR.

\end{itemize}

Every experiment here runs on one laptop, which sets the ceiling on the last point and is stated
plainly in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} rather than left for a reader to infer.
