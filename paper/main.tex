  \ruledtitle{Predicting the Spread of an RL Post-Training Result\\
  from a Single Run}
  \vspace{6pt}
  {\centering
    \normalsize Mehmet Demir G\"uven$^{\dagger}$\par\vspace{2pt}
    \normalsize\textbf{ETH Z\"urich}\par\vspace{1pt}
    \small\texttt{dgueven@student.ethz.ch}\par
  }
  \vspace{6pt}
  \begin{minipage}{\textwidth}
  \begin{center}\textbf{Abstract}\end{center}
  \vspace{-4pt}
  \begin{quote}
  Reinforcement learning post-training is reported from single runs, and how much a rerun would
  move the number is established the only way it currently can be: by repeating the training. We
  show that a run already contains enough information to forecast its own spread.

  The correction that makes this possible is to stop treating training noise as accumulating. A
  perturbation entering at one update is passed through every later update and reweighted by them,
  so the covariance of a run about its mean obeys $S_{t+1} = A_t S_t A_t^{\!\top} + Q_t$. Measuring
  the spectrum of $A_t$ shows what that reweighting is and what it is not: only a third to a half of
  directions contract, the leading one expands by one to two percent per update, and among the
  contracting directions the timescales run from tens of updates to effectively unbounded. It is a
  filter, not a contraction, and no single number stands in for it. Carrying the gradient of the reported metric
  backwards along one stored trajectory then gives the across-seed variance of that metric as a sum
  of scalars, one per update, with no covariance matrix formed. The transpose the backward pass
  needs is free: the mean update of a count-based estimator is the gradient of a scalar potential,
  so its Jacobian is symmetric.

  We test the forecast prospectively --- one run trained and frozen, the prediction recorded, and
  only then the remaining seeds trained. Across $48$ settings spanning a $30\times$ range of
  spreads its median absolute error against the realised standard deviation is $1.08\times$, its worst
  is $1.67\times$, and it lands inside the measured $95\%$ interval in $36$ of them. The underlying covariance law predicts policy
  divergence to $9\%$ at the median where it can be checked exactly; ignoring the filter is wrong
  by up to $3.9\times$ and compressing it to a single timescale by two orders of magnitude. The
  filtering is caused by learning, not by proximity: replacing every reward with a coin flip
  leaves noise, batches and step sizes untouched and divergence then grows $10.7\times$ over eighty
  updates instead of staying flat.

  A caution comes out of the same framework. At $7$B on a task the base model already mostly
  passes, twenty updates collapse the response distribution: every held-out prompt then scores
  exactly $0$ or $1$, two seeds report the same number to the last decimal, and their policies still
  differ. A zero measured spread on a saturated benchmark says the metric has stopped resolving the
  procedure, not that the procedure is reproducible.

  Two readings follow. Since $Q_t \propto \Sigma_t/P$, run length does not enter and batch
  composition is the lever, with square-root returns. And restricting $\Sigma_t$ to one of its
  terms attributes the spread by origin: which responses were sampled dominates which prompts were
  drawn at every group size we measure. Supporting all of it is an exact finite-$G$ covariance for
  the advantage estimators in current use, which is what makes the injected noise measurable and
  which separately fixes the compute-optimal group size.
    \end{quote}
  \end{minipage}
  \vspace{8pt}

    trained afterwards. Bars are bootstrap intervals over runs; the solid line is equality and the
    dotted lines a factor of $1.5$. \textbf{Right:} predicted against measured policy divergence
    for three models of where training noise goes. Propagating the covariance tracks it; treating
    noise as accumulating is wrong by up to $3.9\times$; compressing the filter to one
    timescale is wrong by two orders of magnitude.}

  \end{minipage}
