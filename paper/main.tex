\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards samples at two levels -- prompts, and responses per
prompt -- and every run must decide how to split a fixed rollout budget between them and how large a
step the result supports. Both are currently tuned. We show they can be measured. For binary
rewards we give the exact finite-$G$ mean and covariance of the estimators in current use, from
which two facts follow: the family differs only through a weight $\lambda(p, G)$ on prompt
difficulty, so that variants with a $p$-independent $\lambda$ are the same estimator up to a step
size; and the gradient noise decomposes into a between-prompt term $\tau_b$ that responses cannot
reduce and a within-prompt term $\tau_w$ that shrinks as $1/(G-1)$ and equals, up to one scalar, the
mean Bernoulli variance of the pass-rate distribution. A second-order model of the objective turns
out to be a poor guide -- it understates the useful step size at least fourfold against exactly
computed improvement -- while a model of how far the \emph{policy} moves predicts measured KL to
within 4\% over three decades in step size. Building on the latter gives an efficiency
$\rho = 1/(1 + \Bcrit/P)$ that a trainer can read off the KL it already logs, a closed form for the
optimal group size $G^{*} = 1 + \sqrt{\tau_w/\tau_b}$, inflated by
$\sqrt{1 + c_{\mathrm{pre}}/c_{\mathrm{dec}}}$ once prefill sharing is priced in, and an estimator
that recovers both noise terms from the microbatch gradients a trainer already accumulates, at no
extra rollout cost. We validate on exactly enumerable policies, on populations of transformers
trained from scratch as a single batched kernel, and on a pretrained model with real verifiers.
Measured optima are small -- three to six responses per prompt on transformers and on a pretrained model, before hardware is priced in -- and the drift target that maximises
progress is unchanged across an eightfold change in batch size, while transferring the learning rate
tuned at the smallest batch costs 22\% of the achievable gain at the largest. Carrying a whole
recipe further, to a setting with four times the budget, we find the group-size prediction still
holds -- it wins by 11\% against a predicted 10\% -- while the drift target no longer transfers,
and a tuned constant learning rate beats the best constant drift, which says the optimal drift
schedule is not flat. All experiments run on one laptop, which is the point: the claim is that small
measurements determine large choices.
\end{abstract}
