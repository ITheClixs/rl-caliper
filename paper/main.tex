  \ruledtitle{Predicting the Spread of an RL Post-Training Result\\
  from a Single Run}
  \vspace{6pt}
  {\centering
    \normalsize Mehmet Demir G\"uven$^{\dagger}$\par\vspace{2pt}
    \normalsize\textbf{ETH Z\"urich}\par\vspace{1pt}
    \small\texttt{dgueven@student.ethz.ch}\par
  }
  \vspace{6pt}
  \begin{minipage}{\textwidth}
  \begin{center}\textbf{Abstract}\end{center}
  \vspace{-4pt}
  \begin{quote}
  Reinforcement learning post-training is reported from single runs, and how much a rerun would
  move the number is established the only way it currently can be: by repeating the training. We
  show that a run already contains enough information to forecast its own spread.

  The correction that makes this possible is to stop treating training noise as accumulating. A
  perturbation entering at one update is passed through every later update and reweighted by them,
  so the covariance of a run about its mean obeys $S_{t+1} = A_t S_t A_t^{\!\top} + Q_t$. Measuring
  the spectrum of $A_t$ shows this is a filter and not a contraction: only a third to a half of
  directions contract, the leading direction expands, and the contracting timescales span tens of
  updates to effectively unbounded, so no single number stands in for it. Carrying the gradient of
  the reported metric backwards along one stored trajectory then gives the across-seed variance of
  that metric as a sum of scalars, one per update, with no covariance matrix formed. The transpose
  the backward pass needs is free: the mean update of a count-based estimator is the gradient of a
  scalar potential, so its Jacobian is symmetric.

  We test the forecast prospectively --- one run trained and frozen, the prediction recorded, and
  only then the remaining seeds trained. Across $\numForecastSettings$ settings spanning two policy shapes, two
  prompt-pool diversities and a $\numForecastSpreadRange\times$ range of spreads, its median absolute error against the
  realised standard deviation is $\numForecastMedian\times$, its worst is $\numForecastWorst\times$, and it lands inside the
  measured $95\%$ interval in $\numForecastInside$ of them; the covariance law underneath predicts policy
  divergence to $9\%$ at the median, against $3.9\times$ for ignoring the filter and two orders of
  magnitude for compressing it to one timescale. The filtering is caused by learning, not by
  proximity: replacing every reward with a coin flip leaves the rollouts, the batches and the step
  sizes untouched, and divergence then grows by a factor of $\numNullCoin$ over eighty updates instead of
  ending at $\numNullLearning$ of where it started.

  Two consequences and one caution. Since $Q_t \propto \Sigma_t/P$, run length does not enter and
  batch composition is the lever, with square-root returns; restricting $\Sigma_t$ to one of its
  terms attributes the spread by origin, and which responses were sampled dominates which prompts
  were drawn at every group size we measure. The caution is that at $7$B on a task the base model
  already mostly passes, twenty updates collapse the response distribution: every held-out prompt
  then scores exactly $0$ or $1$, two seeds report the same number to the last decimal, and their
  policies still differ. A zero measured spread on a saturated benchmark says the metric has stopped
  resolving the procedure, not that the procedure is reproducible.
    \end{quote}
  \end{minipage}
  \vspace{8pt}
  \begin{minipage}{\textwidth}
    \centering
    {\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/teaser.pdf}}
    \captionof{figure}{\textbf{Left:} the across-seed standard deviation of the reported pass
    rate, forecast from a single frozen run, against the value realised over $64$ independent runs
    trained afterwards. Bars are bootstrap intervals over runs; the solid line is equality and the
    dotted lines a factor of $1.5$. \textbf{Right:} predicted against measured policy divergence
    for three models of where training noise goes. Propagating the covariance tracks it; treating
    noise as accumulating is wrong by up to $3.9\times$; compressing the filter to one
    timescale is wrong by two orders of magnitude.}
    \label{fig:teaser}
  \end{minipage}
