Title:
  Predicting the Spread of an RL Post-Training Result from a Single Run

Authors:
  Mehmet Demir Guven (ETH Zurich)

Abstract (plain text, as pasted into the submission form):
  Reinforcement learning post-training is reported from single runs, and how much a rerun
  would move the number is established the only way it currently can be: by repeating the
  training. We show that a run already contains enough information to forecast its own
  spread. The correction that makes this possible is to stop treating training noise as
  accumulating. A perturbation entering at one update is passed through every later update
  and reweighted by them, so the covariance of a run about its mean obeys
  S_{t+1} = A_t S_t A_t^T + Q_t. Measuring the spectrum of A_t shows what that reweighting
  is and what it is not: only a third to a half of directions contract, the leading
  direction expands rather than contracts, and among the contracting directions the
  timescales run from tens of updates to effectively unbounded. It is a filter, not a
  contraction, and no single number stands in for it. Carrying the gradient of the reported
  metric backwards along one stored trajectory then gives the across-seed variance of that
  metric as a sum of scalars, one per update, with no covariance matrix formed. The
  transpose the backward pass needs is free: the mean update of a count-based estimator is
  the gradient of a scalar potential, so its Jacobian is symmetric. We test the forecast
  prospectively -- one run trained and frozen, the prediction recorded, and only then the
  remaining seeds trained. Across 48 settings spanning a 30x range of spreads its median
  absolute error is 1.08x and it lands inside the measured 95% interval in 36 of them. The
  filtering is caused by learning, not by proximity: replacing every reward with a coin flip
  leaves noise, batches and step sizes untouched and divergence then grows 10.7x over eighty
  updates instead of staying flat. A caution comes out of the same framework: at 7B on a
  task the base model already mostly passes, twenty updates collapse the response
  distribution, two seeds report the same number to the last decimal, and their policies
  still differ. A zero measured spread on a saturated benchmark says the metric has stopped
  resolving the procedure, not that the procedure is reproducible.
