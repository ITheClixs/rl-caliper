\section{On pretrained models}
\label{sec:real}

Run end to end on Qwen2.5-0.5B with LoRA, the cheap estimator \eqref{eq:cheap} is a factor of
twenty-five short of the measured spread and the transported one sixteen. On Qwen2.5-7B under Adam,
with the recursion lifted to the optimiser state $z = (\theta, m, v)$, it is short by between
$\numAdamScaleShortfallLow$ and $\numAdamScaleShortfallHigh$. Carrying that state matters: on the
exact models, ignoring it costs $24.6\times$ where the lift gives $1.10\times$.

\paragraph{Accuracy is regime-dependent, not uniformly poor.} A corpus whose reward groups go
unanimous injects $\Sigma_t = 0$ and carries no seed noise to forecast, so we built four corpora at
pre-specified mean live-group probabilities and ran the prospective test on each. The errors are
$\numSweepErrors$; the best, on the band at $\numSweepWinBand$, meets a criterion of $1.5\times$
declared before the sweep ran, forecasting $\numSweepWinForecast$ against a measured
$\numSweepWinMeasured$ $[\numSweepWinLow, \numSweepWinHigh]$ on a gain of $\numSweepWinGain$. So
local covariance forecasting can be quantitatively accurate on a pretrained model, though not
reliably so.

All four sit inside the $\numTolLooseBreakeven\times$ break-even of Section~\ref{sec:sizing}, the worst
of them only barely. That is the sense in which these results are usable: not that the forecast is
accurate on pretrained models, but that where we have measured it, it is accurate enough for the
decision it is meant to serve. We have not run the sizing benchmark itself at this scale, and that
is the gap between what we show and what we claim.

\paragraph{The sizing benchmark at this scale is underpowered.} We trained
$\numRealSizeRecipes$ recipes on one corpus at a matched rollout budget of $64$ per update,
crossing $(P,G) \in \{(16,4),(8,8)\}$ with three learning rates spanning a factor of four:
$\numRealSizeRuns$ training runs. The forecast holds up, at a median error of
$\numRealSizeError\times$ and a worst of $\numRealSizeErrorWorst\times$, all six inside the
break-even, tracking $3.0\times$ of the $4.7\times$ spread in measured deviation across recipes.

The benchmark does not. Only $\numRealSizePairs$ of $\numRealSizeAllPairs$ pairs are ordered
unambiguously at $\numRealSizeMargin$ standard errors, because the recipes differ in mean by at
most $0.025$ while their deviations run from $0.010$ to $0.049$: most are genuine near-ties at
sixteen seeds, which is the paper's premise appearing in its own data. On four comparisons a
stopping rule beats a fixed budget by $\numRealSizeFixedGain$
$[\numRealSizeFixedLo, \numRealSizeFixedHi]$ and nothing else separates, the ordering among the
$\sigma$-aware policies changing with the seed cap. That the measured deviation itself comes out
nominally behind the forecast, at $\numRealSizeOracleGain\times$, marks the resolution floor rather
than a result. The $\numSizeFixedGain\times$ of Section~\ref{sec:sizing} is therefore not
demonstrated at this scale and we do not claim it.

\paragraph{Two things the sweep falsified.} It does not support a monotone relationship between
stochastic support and accuracy: the ordering of errors across the four bands does not follow the
ordering of their live-group probabilities. And a one-run curvature diagnostic, calibrated on the
exact models to refuse forecasts whose runs are not small perturbations of each other, does not
transfer: it refuses every pretrained run, including the accurate one.

\paragraph{What survives is narrower than the magnitude.} The share of prompts whose group is not
unanimous falls from about a half to under $5\%$ by the fifth update, which is measured rather than
modelled, so the run stops injecting seed noise there. The failed forecast's own kernel puts
$\numRealFirstHalfShare\%$ of its predicted variance before that point; whether the actual seed
variance is distributed the same way we did not test.

\paragraph{A metric can go blind before the seeds agree.} At $7$B on a task the base model already
mostly passes, twenty updates collapse the response distribution: every held-out prompt scores
exactly $0$ or $1$, two seeds report the same number to the last decimal, and their policies still
differ by up to $8\times10^{-2}$ in KL. A zero measured spread on a saturated benchmark is not
evidence of a reproducible procedure, and it is the failure mode that a practitioner is most likely
to read as success.
